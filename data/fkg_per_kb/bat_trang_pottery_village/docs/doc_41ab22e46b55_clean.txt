As we journey down the Red River, the alluvial plain unfolds, revealing the tranquil yet vibrant Bát Tràng Pottery Village, a heritage coordinate nestled in Gia Lâm District, on the outskirts of Hanoi. This ancient settlement, born from the ashes of the Lý Dynasty, when the imperial court relocated to Thăng Long, has been a hub of ceramic craftsmanship for centuries. The name Bát Tràng, meaning "the great courtyard" dedicated to pottery, was forged during this era, as artisans from the Bồ Bát region sailed down to this fertile plain to establish their livelihood.

The village's rustic reddish-brown hue, blending with the rough wall surface, immediately catches the eye, as the colossal architectural block rises up with undulating spirals like a potter's wheel. The unique identifying feature is the intertwined spiral cone blocks with gaps soaring straight up to the sky, majestically standing by the tranquil canal, adjacent to the main road bustling with vehicles full of ceramics passing by.

As we wander through the village, the air is filled with the sweet scent of clay and the soft glow of kilns, as artisans tirelessly work day and night by their pottery wheels, their hands moving in perfect harmony with the earth and fire. Visitors enthusiastically join in, hand-molding raw clay batches or strolling along small alleys filled with drying charcoal, immersing themselves in the ancient craft.

On major festival days, the entire village comes alive, as the sacred water procession ritual from the main river to the đình takes place, filling the air with the sound of drums and chanting. As we touch a cool, indigo-glazed vase, we are suddenly enveloped in an unusual sense of peace, not just admiration for the craftsmanship, but also a profound emotion upon realizing that every inert lump of clay can transform into a soul under the flame of generational passion.

Bat Trang Pottery Village is considered the oldest ceramic making village in Vietnam, with records dating back to the 14th or 15th century, although some believe it may have appeared even earlier. The village's ceramics, renowned for their beauty and durability, were not only favored in the domestic market but also exported to foreign lands, thanks to its strategic location beside the Red River, between Thang Long and Pho Hien, two ancient trade centers in the north of Vietnam during the 15th-17th century.

As we explore this living museum, we are transported to a world where tradition meets innovation, where the past and present converge, and where the art of pottery becomes a testament to the enduring spirit of the Vietnamese people.
