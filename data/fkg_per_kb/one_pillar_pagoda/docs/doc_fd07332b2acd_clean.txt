The pagoda's unique structure and spiritual significance make it one of the most popular tourist attractions in Hanoi.

Situated within the grounds behind the Temple of Literature, near the Ho Chi Minh Mausoleum and close to the West Lake, the One Pillar Pagoda is easily accessible for visitors. It is open daily from 7 AM to 6 PM, offering free admission to Vietnamese citizens while charging a modest fee for foreign tourists. The pagoda's serene atmosphere and historical importance draw many visitors who often combine their visit with tours of nearby landmarks such as the Temple of Literature and the Ho Chi Minh Mausoleum.

The One Pillar Pagoda, also known as Chùa Một Cột, stands as a unique symbol of cultural and architectural significance in Hanoi, Vietnam. This iconic structure, nestled within the Ba Đình district, is not only a testament to the rich history and spiritual heritage of the region but also a revered site for both locals and tourists alike. The pagoda's distinctive design, inspired by the image of a blooming lotus flower rising from water, embodies the purity and nobility of Buddhist teachings. Officially recognized by the Asia Book of Records in 2012 as the most uniquely structured temple in Asia, the One Pillar Pagoda continues to captivate visitors with its intricate architecture and profound cultural importance.

Located adjacent to the Ho Chi Minh Mausoleum and Ba Dinh Square, the pagoda's opening hours align with those of these nearby landmarks, welcoming visitors daily from 7 AM to 6 PM. For Vietnamese visitors, entry is free, while foreign tourists are required to pay a modest admission fee of 25,000 Vietnamese Dong. The pagoda's serene setting amidst the bustling cityscape offers a tranquil retreat, where one can immerse themselves in the beauty and tranquility of this historic site.

The origins of the One Pillar Pagoda trace back to the reign of King Ly Thai Tong in 1049. According to legend, the king had a dream in which he was granted a lotus flower by the Bodhisattva Avalokitesvara. Upon waking, he shared his vision with his advisors, who suggested constructing a temple to honor this divine gift. Thus, the pagoda was built with a single wooden column supporting a lotus-shaped pavilion, symbolizing the lotus flower from the king's dream. Over time, the temple underwent several renovations, particularly during the Tran, Later Le, and Nguyen dynasties, each contributing to its enduring legacy.

In 1954, the French forces attempted to destroy the pagoda before their withdrawal from Hanoi, leading to significant damage. However, in 1955, the government undertook a reconstruction effort under the guidance of architect Nguyen Ba Lang, restoring the pagoda to its original form. Today, the pagoda retains much of its ancient charm, with its architectural elements reflecting the meticulous craftsmanship of past artisans.

The One Pillar Pagoda's architecture is characterized by its singular column, which rises from a pond, creating a striking visual effect reminiscent of a lotus flower emerging from water. The column itself is composed of two stacked stone blocks, with part of it submerged beneath the water and the rest extending above the surface to a height of four meters. The diameter of the column is approximately 1.2 meters, providing a sturdy foundation for the entire structure.

Surrounding the column is a square platform adorned with railings, known as the Lotus Platform. Inside this platform, the interior is lavishly decorated with a golden statue of the Thousand-Armed Avalokitesvara, along with various offerings such as ceramic vases, incense burners, and tea sets. The altar is intricately adorned with cloud motifs and gold leaf, enhancing the sacred atmosphere of the space. Above the altar, a small plaque inscribed with the words "Lotus Platform" in gold against a red background adds to the pagoda's aesthetic appeal.

The roof of the pagoda is covered with red-tiled tiles that have aged gracefully over time, lending an air of antiquity to the structure. Each tile is meticulously crafted, showcasing the skill and dedication of the artisans who created them. Atop the roof, the dragon motif facing the full moon represents the harmony between yin and yang, symbolizing fertility and growth. This decorative element is a hallmark of traditional Vietnamese temple architecture, imbuing the pagoda with a sense of cultural continuity and spiritual significance.

Regular ceremonies are held at the pagoda on the first and fifteenth days of each lunar month, when the management team conducts cleaning rituals and performs offerings to maintain the sanctity of the site. These events provide visitors with a glimpse into the ongoing religious practices that have sustained the pagoda's spiritual life for centuries.

Since 1962, the One Pillar Pagoda has been designated as a National Historical and Architectural Monument, recognizing its invaluable contribution to Vietnam's cultural heritage. Its designation as the most uniquely structured temple in Asia by the Asia Book of Records in 2012 further underscores its global significance. The pagoda remains a place of worship and pilgrimage, where devotees come to seek blessings and reflect on the timeless values of Buddhism.

The One Pillar Pagoda's history is deeply intertwined with the spiritual journey of King Ly Thai Tong, who was a devout follower of Buddhism and the Vô Ngôn Thông sect. During his reign, numerous statues of Buddha were restored, and more than ninety-five new temples were constructed, reflecting the flourishing of Buddhism at that time. On major Buddhist holidays, the king would often grant tax exemptions throughout the country as a gesture of goodwill towards the people.
