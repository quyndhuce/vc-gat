The One Pillar Pagoda, formally known as Diên Hựu tự, is a historic Buddhist temple located in the central Ba Đình district of Hanoi, Vietnam, near the Thăng Long Citadel. The pagoda's most distinctive feature is its unique structure, known as Liên Hoa Đài or "the lotus pedestal," which consists of a building perched on a single pillar, symbolizing purity in Buddhism. The original pagoda was constructed in 1049 during the reign of Emperor Lý Thái Tông, who ruled from 1028 to 1054. According to legend, Lý Thái Tông was childless until he had a dream where the Bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara presented him with a son while sitting on a lotus flower. Following this dream, Lý Thái Tông married a peasant girl and they had a son. To express his gratitude, the emperor built the pagoda in 1049, following the advice of a monk named Thiền Tuệ, who suggested constructing a temple on a single pillar in a lotus pond, mirroring the scene from the emperor’s dream.

Initially, the pagoda was situated in the Tây Cấm Garden in Thạch Bảo, Vĩnh Thuận district, within the ancient capital city of Thăng Long, now modern-day Hanoi. Before its official opening, the pagoda served as a venue for prayers aimed at ensuring the longevity of the monarch. During the Lý dynasty, the temple hosted an annual royal ceremony on Vesak, the birth anniversary of Gautama Buddha. Monarchs would conduct a Buddha-bathing ritual, attracting both monks and laypeople. As part of the ceremony, the monarch would release a bird, a tradition followed by the attendees.

In 1105, Emperor Lý Nhân Tông undertook renovations to enhance the pagoda's beauty, including the excavation of a lotus-shaped lake named Linh Chiểu Lake. Surrounding the lake, a corridor was constructed, beyond which another lake, Bích Trì Lake, was dug, along with bridges to facilitate passage. Additionally, a stupa was erected in front of the temple courtyard. However, an attempt to install a large bell in 1109 failed due to its size and weight, leading to its relocation to a nearby field, where it became known as Chuông Quy Điền or "bell of the turtles' field." Unfortunately, during the Ming dynasty's invasion and occupation of Vietnam in the early 15th century, the commanding general of the retreating Ming forces ordered the bell to be melted down for weapon production.

After the Lý dynasty, the pagoda underwent several restorations, notably during the Nguyễn dynasty in the periods between 1840-1850 and 1922. In 1954, the pagoda was dynamited, contrary to popular belief, not by the French but by a Vietnamese lieutenant of the French army, who faced severe punishment for this act. Following the incident, the Ministry of Culture of the Democratic Republic of Vietnam undertook the task of restoring the pagoda and its Lotus Station in 1955, adhering to the architectural style of the Nguyễn dynasty.

In recent years, efforts have been made to reconstruct the original architecture of the Diên Hựu Pagoda. In 2020, a team of researchers, architects, and painters proposed a plan to restore the pagoda based on the architectural style of the Lý dynasty. Their proposal centered around the hexagonal structure of the One Pillar Pagoda, resembling a lotus flower, and utilized 3D images and virtual reality to present their findings.
