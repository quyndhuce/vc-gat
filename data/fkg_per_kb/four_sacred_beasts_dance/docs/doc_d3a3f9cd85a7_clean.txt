The Four Sacred Beasts Dance, a vibrant and culturally significant tradition, has been a cherished part of Vietnamese festive celebrations for centuries. This dance, which draws its inspiration from the four sacred beasts—Thanh Long (Azure Dragon), Bạch Hổ (White Tiger), Huyền Vũ (Black Tortoise), and Chu Tước (Red Phoenix)—embodies the essence of ancient beliefs and traditions. These mythical creatures symbolize the four cardinal directions and the elements of fire, water, earth, and wind, respectively, reflecting a deep connection to the natural world and celestial bodies.

Originating from the ancient capital of Thang Long, the Four Sacred Beasts Dance was initially performed at the imperial court during various ceremonies under the Nguyen dynasty. It was later adapted into a more widespread folk tradition, celebrated during festivals and community gatherings. The dance is not only a form of entertainment but also a spiritual practice that brings blessings and harmony to the land and its people.

The preparation of the costumes for the dancers is a meticulous process that involves crafting elaborate headpieces and body coverings. The heads of the dragons, tigers, tortoises, and phoenixes are made from paperboard, intricately painted with vibrant colors and adorned with decorative elements. The bodies are fashioned from silk or brocade, embroidered with metallic threads and colorful yarns, creating a dazzling display of traditional craftsmanship. Each dancer dons these elaborate costumes, embodying the spirit of their respective beast through graceful and dynamic movements.

The performance itself is a harmonious blend of music and movement, guided by the rhythms of traditional eight-tone music. There are no lyrics sung along with the dance; instead, the dancers move in sync with the musical beats, executing a series of choreographed gestures that reflect the characteristics of each animal. The azure dragon's fluidity, the white tiger's strength, the black tortoise's stability, and the red phoenix's elegance are all conveyed through precise and expressive motions.

In modern times, the tradition of the Four Sacred Beasts Dance continues to thrive, particularly in rural areas where it remains an integral part of local culture. One notable example is the village of Huong Son in My Duc district, Hanoi, which boasts three teams dedicated to this art form. Notably, the team at Den Duc Khe temple comprises exclusively elderly performers who have dedicated their lives to preserving this heritage. According to Mr. Pham Van Hai, a member of the Duc Khe team, the tradition was introduced by General Cao Viet Minh during the Hung kings' era, teaching villagers not only agricultural practices but also the art of the Four Sacred Beasts Dance. This legacy has been passed down through generations, with each new cohort learning from their elders to ensure the continuity of this cultural treasure.

During the Lunar New Year festivities, the teams are especially active, performing throughout the villages and at significant sites such as the Temple of Literature and Chua Huong (Perfume Pagoda). The dancers can be seen in action from the fifth day of the lunar new year onwards, participating in various temple fairs and community events until the end of the first lunar month. The Duc Khe team, in particular, has gained recognition beyond their local area, having been invited to perform at numerous festivals across different provinces including Nam Dinh, Thanh Hoa, Hoa Binh, Vinh Phuc, and Ninh Binh.

The oldest member of the Duc Khe team, Mr. Pham Van Thuong, aged 87, exemplifies the dedication and passion that drives these performances. Despite his advanced age, he continues to participate, playing a crucial role in providing musical accompaniment for the dancers. His commitment to preserving the ancient dances and bringing joy to audiences during the festive season is a testament to the enduring spirit of the Four Sacred Beasts Dance.

Each team member plays a vital role in maintaining the tradition, whether through performing, making costumes, or passing on skills to younger generations. For instance, Mr. Pham Van Hai, aged 68, carries on the legacy of his father, ensuring that the intricate costumes are crafted according to traditional methods. These costumes take nearly a month to complete, reflecting the time-intensive nature of the craft. The dancers also train rigorously, often practicing together for one or two weeks before joining the main group to perform at various events.

The physical demands of the dance are considerable, requiring stamina and flexibility. For example, Mr. Trinh Van Binh, aged 70, is responsible for controlling the dragon's head, a task that requires significant strength and agility. He explains that the dragon's head, weighing between 15 to 20 kilograms, must be maneuvered skillfully to create lifelike movements, enhancing the visual appeal of the performance.

The Four Sacred Beasts Dance not only showcases the artistic talents of the performers but also fosters a sense of community and cultural pride. As the dancers weave through the streets and temples, they bring joy and festivity to the people, reinforcing the bonds within the community and celebrating the rich cultural heritage of Vietnam. Through their tireless efforts, the dancers ensure that this ancient tradition remains alive and vibrant, connecting past and present generations in a shared celebration of cultural identity and communal spirit.

The Four Sacred Beasts Dance, a traditional dance ensemble deeply rooted in Vietnamese culture, has been performed in the land known as "Ngũ phụng tề phi" for over a century. This ancient dance, which includes the mythical creatures Lân (ox), Long (dragon), Quy (turtle), and Phụng (phoenix), has captivated audiences with its vibrant performances and rich cultural significance. Over time, the once bustling scenes of the dance have faded into the memories of the elderly, leaving behind only cherished recollections of its former glory.

Among the four characters, Lân is the most beloved, often captivating the audience with its dynamic movements and lively presence. The dance's origins trace back to a time when it was first introduced by a group led by Master Nhung, who later passed the torch to his son, Master Nha, after his passing. Following the historic victory at Dien Bien Phu in 1954, Master Nha organized a grand performance of the Four Sacred Beasts Dance to celebrate this significant event, marking the expansion of the dance to various regions within the province.

Despite the passage of time and the challenges faced by the dance, the original choreography and traditions have gradually diminished. Today, only a few senior artists still remember parts of the dance, each preserving fragments of the intricate routines that once defined the performance. These artists, such as Mr. Đinh Quýt and Mr. Nguyễn Huệ, continue to pass down their knowledge, ensuring that the essence of the Four Sacred Beasts Dance remains alive.

The musical accompaniment for the dance is a blend of traditional instruments including the nhị (a type of flute), sáo (flute), kèn (trumpet), and chập chõa (a small drum). The primary instruments used are the trống (drum) and phèng la (a large gong), which create the rhythmic beats that drive the dance forward. To enhance the atmosphere and highlight the characteristics of each character, the artists have devised a unique form of firework called pháo xì, which produces smoke and flame without causing an explosion. This special effect is created using a mixture of diêm tiêu (matches) and tro cây dâu (mulberry ash) or tro cây sắn (cassava ash), carefully blended in proportions that reflect the personality of each character.

During performances, the stage lights flicker as the dancers emerge from the backstage area. A loud signal from the pháo xì marks the beginning of the dance, followed by the appearance of the Lân character, who emerges confidently to the beat of the drums, his head adorned with a flaming horn that spews smoke and fire. Each character then takes center stage, showcasing their unique movements and engaging the audience with their distinctive visual elements. The crowd roars with excitement, the festive atmosphere intensifying with every moment.

One of the most popular segments of the Four Sacred Beasts Dance is the "Lân mẫu xuất Lân nhi" (Mother Lân giving birth to Child Lân). In one memorable performance, Mr. Đinh Quýt played the role of Child Lân, lying on his back while being held by the performer playing Mother Lân. During a particularly enthusiastic performance, the two performers holding Mother Lân became so engrossed in the dance that they accidentally dropped Child Lân onto the ground beside a fake bush. The audience watched in anticipation as Mother Lân pretended to go through labor pains, but Child Lân did not appear. Sensing the tension, the organizers quickly covered the stage lights, allowing Child Lân to run out onto the stage and rejoin Mother Lân, much to the delight of the audience.

In 1990, with financial support from a businessman, Mr. Đinh Quýt and Mr. Nguyễn Huệ established a new troupe of dancers in Đà Nẵng. Over the next three years, the troupe performed extensively throughout Quảng Nam, especially during the Mid-Autumn Festival, entertaining children and adults alike. The Four Sacred Beasts Dance, with its rich cultural heritage, was warmly received wherever it was performed. Unfortunately, in 1993, a tragic fire destroyed all the props and costumes of the troupe, bringing an abrupt end to their performances.

Today, the legacy of the Four Sacred Beasts Dance continues to be celebrated and preserved by a handful of dedicated artists. Despite the challenges faced by the dance, it remains a testament to the enduring spirit of traditional Vietnamese culture, captivating audiences with its vibrant performances and deep cultural significance.
