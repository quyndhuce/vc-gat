Calligraphy, derived from the Ancient Greek term 'kalligraphía', meaning beautiful writing, is the art of fine handwriting elevated to a visual art form. This intricate craft involves the design and execution of lettering or characters using various writing instruments such as pens, ink brushes, or other tools. Unlike type design and non-classical hand-lettering, classical calligraphy stands distinct, though some practitioners may engage in both disciplines.

In East Asia and the Islamic world, Calligraphy holds a significant place in artistic expression, often reflecting the meaning of the text as a whole or individual words. Modern Western Calligraphy finds application in diverse areas, including wedding invitations, type and computer font design, letter cutting, hand-lettered logo design, religious art, graphic design, commissioned artworks, carved stone inscriptions, and memorial documents. Additionally, it is utilized in theatrical props, moving images for film and television, testimonials, birth and death certificates, maps, and other written works.

In the Western world, the primary tools for Calligraphy are pens and brushes, with nibs that can be flat, round, or pointed. For decorative effects, multi-nib pens or steel brushes are employed. Felt-tip and ballpoint pens, while useful, do not produce angled lines. Gothic script, for instance, requires a stub nib pen. In Asia, calligraphers often use a makta, or penknife, to cut reed pens, which offer a broader range of motion compared to metallic pens. Common tools include quills, dip pens, ink brushes, qalams, fountain pens, chiselled markers, reed pens, and fudepens.

For Calligraphy, ink is typically water-based and less viscous than oil-based ink used in printing. Specialty papers with high ink absorption and uniform texture aid in producing cleaner lines. Parchment or vellum, materials that allow erasure of imperfections and viewing of lines without a lightbox, are frequently used. Lightboxes and templates assist in achieving straight lines without leaving pencil marks. Ruled paper, often spaced at intervals of one-quarter or one-half inch, is another option, though one-inch spaces are occasionally used, particularly with Uncial script, characterized by inch-high letters.

In East Asia, Calligraphy is deeply revered and remains an integral part of contemporary traditional culture. Chinese Calligraphy, known as shūfǎ or fǎshū, Japanese as shodō, and Korean as seoye, reflects the importance of this art form. The earliest known Chinese characters, oracle bone script, were carved on ox scapulae and tortoise plastrons during the Shang dynasty for divination purposes. These inscriptions were later interpreted and carved directly onto the shells or bones. As scripts evolved, the bronzeware script and large seal script emerged, exemplified by the Mao Gong ding, a bronze vessel with over 500 inscribed characters.

During the Imperial era, the graphs on old steles have been preserved, dating back to around 200 BCE and written in the small seal script style. Emperor Qin Shi Huang, who unified China, imposed reforms that included standardizing characters into a set of 3300 small seal characters. Although the brush was the main writing implement, few papers from this period have survived, and examples of this style are primarily found on steles. The clerical script, more regularized and resembling modern text, was also authorized during his reign.
