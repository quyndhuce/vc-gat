The Bronze Age is a period in the development of civilization when advanced metallurgical techniques, at least in systematic and widespread use, primarily involve smelting copper and tin from naturally occurring outcrops and then alloying these metals to create bronze. This era forms part of a tripartite system for prehistoric societies, following the Neolithic Stone Age in some regions. However, in many parts of Sub-Saharan Africa, the Neolithic Stone Age was succeeded by the Iron Age without an intervening Bronze Age.

The origins of the Bronze Age are a subject of debate, with the technique of making bronze possibly discovered independently in various regions. The necessary conditions for ancient people to discover bronze include the presence of high-grade open-pit copper mines, often accompanied by native copper, located on mountain slopes or in caves suitable for shelter. Primitive inhabitants living there would burn wood for warmth, generating high temperatures that reduced copper from its ores. Initially, they might have been fearful of the strange "stone" left behind on cave floors, but over generations, they realized it was not only harmless but also useful for toolmaking.

In Southeast Asia, there are such open-pit copper mines, including the large Letpadaung mine near Monywa in Myanmar's Sagaing region, and smaller ones in Loei province, northeastern Thailand. This explains why Southeast Asians began casting bronze around 5000 BC. From this point, they mastered metallurgy and recognized the importance of mining, leading to the discovery of other minerals like iron, lead, and zinc. Prehistoric migrations may have facilitated different ethnic groups occupying these mineral-rich areas.

The earliest known tin-bronze artifacts date back to the region now comprising Iran and Iraq, around the end of the fourth millennium BC. There are claims that tin-bronze appeared earlier in Thailand during the fifth millennium BC. Tin-free arsenical bronze was produced in Anatolia and both sides of the Caucasus mountains from the early third millennium BC. Some scholars date certain artifacts from the Maykop culture in North Caucasus to the middle of the fourth millennium BC, making them among the oldest known bronze objects, while others place them in the middle of the third millennium BC.

In the Ancient Near East, the Bronze Age is divided into three main periods:

EBA - Early Bronze Age (around 3500-2000 BC)

MBA - Middle Bronze Age (around 2000-1600 BC)

LBA - Late Bronze Age (around 1600-1200 BC)

Each period can be further subdivided into smaller phases such as EB I, EB II, MB IIa, etc. Metallurgy first developed in Anatolia, now Turkey, where the highlands have rich deposits of copper and tin. Copper was also mined in Cyprus, the Negev desert, Iran, and the Persian Gulf region. Commonly, copper was mixed with arsenic, leading to long-distance trade routes within and beyond Anatolia due to the increasing demand for tin. Pure copper was imported via sea routes to the Mesopotamian empires.

The Early Bronze Age saw an increase in urbanization and the formation of organized state-like structures, along with the invention of writing (the Uruk period in the fifth millennium BC). During the Middle Bronze Age, human migration altered the political landscape of the Near East (Amorites, Hittites, Hurrians, Hyksos, and possibly Israelites). The Late Bronze Age was characterized by powerful competing kingdoms and their vassal states (Assyria, Babylonia, Hittites, Mitanni).
