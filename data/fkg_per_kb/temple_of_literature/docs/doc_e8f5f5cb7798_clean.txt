The Temple of Literature, also known as Van Mieu-Quoc Tu Giam, is a revered site located at Number 58, Quoc Tu Giam Street, Van Mieu Ward, Dong Da District, Hanoi. This historic complex opens daily from 8:00 AM to 5:00 PM, offering visitors a glimpse into Vietnam's rich educational heritage. The Temple of Literature stands as a testament to the country’s deep-rooted tradition of scholarship and reverence for learning, making it an essential destination for anyone exploring the thousand-year-old cultural legacy of Hanoi.

The temple is situated amidst four major streets: Quoc Tu Giam, Van Mieu, Nguyen Thai Hoc, and Ton Duc Thang, creating a serene and culturally significant setting. From above, the sprawling grounds of the Temple of Literature reveal a meticulously preserved ensemble of ancient structures, each telling a story of its own. The temple’s significance extends beyond its physical presence, embodying the spirit of academic excellence that has been a cornerstone of Vietnamese society for centuries.

Established in 1070 during the reign of King Ly Thanh Tong, the Temple of Literature was initially built as a place to honor Confucius, his disciples, and other esteemed scholars. In 1076, under King Ly Nhan Tong, the adjacent structure known as Quoc Tu Giam was added, serving as the first national university in Vietnam. Over time, this institution evolved, adapting to the changing needs of the nation. During the Tran Dynasty, it was renamed the National Academy, broadening its scope to include not only the children of nobility but also those from distinguished families. By the time of the Le Dynasty, particularly during the reign of King Le Thanh Tong, the practice of erecting steles to commemorate successful candidates who passed the imperial examinations became commonplace.

Throughout its history, the Temple of Literature has undergone numerous transformations. During the Nguyen Dynasty, the institution moved to Hue, while the original site in Hanoi retained its importance as a cultural landmark. The temple was later reconstructed, with the addition of the Khai Thanh Shrine to honor the parents of Confucius. However, the site suffered significant damage during the early years of the French colonial period when a bombardment in 1947 led to the collapse of several buildings, leaving behind only the foundation and two stone columns.

Today, the Temple of Literature encompasses a vast area of over 54,300 square meters, featuring a variety of architectural wonders. The complex is enclosed by high walls, providing a sense of seclusion and reverence. At the heart of the temple lies the Grand Middle Gate (Dai Trung Mon), which leads to the Temple of Literature itself. Beyond this gate, one encounters the serene waters of the Lake of Literature (Ho Van), a tranquil body of water that reflects the beauty of the surrounding structures. The lake is flanked by lush greenery, creating a harmonious blend of nature and architecture.

The Temple of Literature is divided into three main areas: the Inner Sanctuary, the Garden of the Temple, and the Outer Area. Each section is designed with meticulous attention to detail, reflecting the traditional Vietnamese architectural style prevalent from the 17th to the 19th century. The Inner Sanctuary, the most sacred part of the complex, is separated from the outer gardens by a series of walls, ensuring a sense of sanctity and reverence. This area is further subdivided into five distinct zones, each marked by ornate gates and courtyards. Notable structures within the Inner Sanctuary include the Temple of Literature Gate, the Grand Middle Gate, the Pavilion of the Constellation of Literary Stars (Khue Van Cac), the Great Success Gate (Dai Thanh Mon), and the Hall of Worship, culminating in the Great Learning Hall (Thai Hoc).

The Pavilion of the Constellation of Literary Stars, a prominent feature of the Temple of Literature, stands as a symbol of academic achievement. It houses 82 steles inscribed with the names of successful candidates from imperial examinations, recognized by UNESCO as part of the World Documentary Heritage. These steles serve as a tangible link to Vietnam’s illustrious past, celebrating the intellectual achievements of generations of scholars.

The Temple of Literature also hosts various cultural events throughout the year, including ceremonies honoring outstanding students and poetry gatherings. During the Lunar New Year, the site becomes a focal point for locals seeking blessings, with many visiting to "ask for characters," hoping for good fortune and prosperity in the coming year.

Visitors to the Temple of Literature can explore a range of attractions, from the serene lakes and gardens to the intricately designed pavilions and halls. The atmosphere is one of tranquility and respect, with each step revealing new insights into the rich cultural heritage of Vietnam. The temple’s architecture, characterized by its symmetrical layout and traditional materials such as ceramic tiles and carved wood, offers a visual feast for the senses. The experience of walking through the temple grounds is enhanced by the detailed craftsmanship evident in every structure, from the ornate gates to the delicate carvings on the steles.

In summary, the Temple of Literature is more than just a historical site; it is a living testament to the enduring value placed on education and scholarly pursuits in Vietnamese culture. Its significance extends far beyond its physical boundaries, inspiring visitors with a sense of awe and reverence for the intellectual achievements of the past and the promise of future generations.

The Temple of Literature, also known as Van Mieu-Quoc Tu Giam, is a significant historical site in Hanoi, Vietnam, embodying the cultural evolution of the nation and its contributions to the Confucian civilization in the region and globally. This complex, located in the southern part of the ancient Thang Long Citadel, comprises two main components: the Temple of Literature dedicated to Confucius and other esteemed Confucian scholars, and the Imperial Academy, the first university in Vietnam. The temple was established in 1070 during the reign of King Ly Thanh Tong, who ordered the construction of statues of Confucius, his four disciples, and other prominent Confucian philosophers for worship and annual ceremonies.

In 1076, under King Ly Nhan Tong, the Imperial Academy was built adjacent to the temple, initially serving only the children of royalty and high-ranking officials. However, in 1253, King Tran Thai Tong expanded the academy's enrollment to include talented commoners, marking a significant shift towards inclusivity. During the reign of King Tran Minh Tong, Chu Van An, a revered teacher and exemplary figure in Vietnamese education, was appointed as the headmaster of the Imperial Academy and tutor to the princes. After his death in 1370, he was honored by King Tran Nghe Tong with a place of reverence beside Confucius at the Temple of Literature.

The Temple of Literature flourished under the Later Le Dynasty when Confucianism was widely practiced. In 1484, King Le Thanh Tong erected steles inscribed with the names of successful candidates from the imperial examinations starting from 1442. These steles, now numbering 82, are considered invaluable relics and were recognized by UNESCO as part of the World Documentary Heritage program in 2010. Following this recognition, the steles became the second documentary heritage site in Vietnam after the Nguyen Dynasty’s wooden block prints.

The layout of the Temple of Literature follows a symmetrical design along a north-south axis, mirroring the overall structure of the Confucian temple. The complex occupies a vast area of 54,331 square meters, surrounded by streets on all sides. The main entrance, known as the Van Mieu Gate, faces south on Quoc Tu Giam Street, while the northern boundary is marked by Nguyen Thai Hoc Street, the western side by Ton Duc Thang Street, and the eastern side by Van Mieu Street.

Upon entering through the main gate, visitors encounter a series of gates and courtyards that lead to the heart of the temple. The first zone begins with the Van Mieu Gate and extends to the Dai Trung Gate, flanked by smaller gates named Thanh Duc and Dat Tai. The second zone continues from the Dai Trung Gate to the Khuê Văn Pavilion, a distinctive architectural feature that symbolizes the city of Hanoi. This pavilion, though not grandiose, boasts harmonious proportions and elegant design, featuring four square columns supporting a wooden upper level with circular windows and simple wooden railings. The roof, composed of two layers of tiles, forms an eight-roofed structure with flat eaves and a smooth roof surface. The pavilion's upper floor is a square tower with eight roofs, adorned with round windows on each side, symbolizing the sun's rays.

On either side of the Khuê Văn Pavilion are the Bi Van and Suc Van gates, which lead to the areas housing the stele monuments. Each stele, carved into stone, commemorates the names of those who passed the imperial exams, including the top three positions of Trang Yuan, Bang Nhan, and Than Hau, as well as the Hoang Giap and Jin Shi ranks. These steles rest on the backs of turtles, representing longevity and wisdom. The steles date back to examinations held between 1442 and 1779, providing a tangible link to Vietnam's educational history.

The third zone includes the Heavenly Light Pond, a square-shaped pond named Thien Quang Tinh, which reflects the sun's light. Surrounding the pond are houses containing the stele monuments, creating a serene and reflective atmosphere. The fourth zone, the central area, houses the Bai Duong Hall and the Thang Cung Hall, the primary structures of the temple. The Bai Duong Hall, an outer hall, serves as a ceremonial space, while the Thang Cung Hall, an inner hall, is a more intimate setting for private rituals and contemplation.

The fifth zone, originally a shrine dedicated to the parents of Confucius but later destroyed, was rebuilt as the Thai Hoc Hall in 1999. This reconstruction aimed to preserve the historical continuity of the site and provide a space for educational activities and cultural events.

Today, the Temple of Literature remains a popular destination for both domestic and international tourists, offering a glimpse into Vietnam's rich cultural heritage and educational traditions. It is also a venue for recognizing outstanding students and hosting annual poetry gatherings during the full moon of the first lunar month. Additionally, it has become a place of pilgrimage for students seeking good fortune before their exams, blending historical reverence with contemporary educational aspirations.

The Temple of Literature stands as a testament to Vietnam's commitment to education and cultural preservation, reflecting the country's deep-rooted respect for learning and intellectual pursuits. Its intricate architecture, historical significance, and ongoing relevance make it a must-visit site for anyone interested in understanding the cultural and educational foundations of Vietnam.
