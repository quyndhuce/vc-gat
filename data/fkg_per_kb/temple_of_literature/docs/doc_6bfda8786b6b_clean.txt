The Temple of Literature, also known as Văn Miếu, is a revered temple dedicated to Confucius located in Hanoi, northern Vietnam. Founded and initially constructed in 1070 during the reign of Emperor Lý Thánh Tông, this historic site has served as a significant cultural and educational hub for over a millennium. The temple's establishment marked the beginning of a long-standing tradition of honoring Confucius and other scholarly figures, alongside hosting the Imperial Academy, or Quốc Tử Giám, from 1076 to 1779. This institution was Vietnam's first national university, educating the country’s elite, including bureaucrats, nobles, and royalty.

The Temple of Literature is one of several such temples in Vietnam, reflecting the profound influence of Confucianism on the nation's intellectual and cultural heritage. Situated to the south of the Imperial Citadel of Thăng Long, the temple encompasses a vast area of over 54,000 square meters, featuring a serene lake, a park, and multiple courtyards enclosed by a brick wall. Upon entering through the Great Gate, visitors encounter four towering pillars flanked by two stelae that command horsemen to dismount, signifying respect for the sacred grounds.

Inside, the temple's layout mirrors that of the temple at Qufu, Confucius' birthplace in China. Three pathways lead through the complex, each designated for different purposes. The central pathway was reserved exclusively for the monarch, while the left and right paths were meant for administrative and military officials, respectively. Above the central path hangs a large bronze bell, which was rung to announce the arrival of important dignitaries. This bell, adorned with intricate designs representing the phoenix and dragon, symbolizes the imperial authority and is a notable feature of the temple.

The temple's interior is divided into five courtyards, each serving distinct functions. The first two courtyards are tranquil spaces filled with ancient trees and manicured lawns, providing a peaceful environment for scholars to study and reflect. The third courtyard, known as the great central courtyard or the courtyard of great success, houses the Khuê Văn pavilion, a distinctive structure built in 1805. This pavilion, supported by 67 white-washed stone stilts, stands as a symbol of modern Hanoi. Its upper level, painted red, features two circular windows and a beautifully crafted roof. Inside, a bronze bell is suspended, intended to be rung on auspicious occasions. The pavilion also contains poetic phrases that celebrate Vietnamese traditional culture.

Throughout its history, the Temple of Literature has undergone numerous reconstructions and restorations, particularly in 1920, 1954, and 2000, ensuring the preservation of its ancient architectural styles and precious relics. Despite periods of conflict and change, the temple remains a testament to Vietnam's rich cultural heritage and enduring commitment to education and scholarship.
