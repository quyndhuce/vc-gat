Hang Quat Street, located in Hoan Kiem District of Hanoi, is a vibrant and historically rich area that stretches for 200 meters and is 8 meters wide. The street runs from Pho Luong Van Can to the intersection with Hang Non and Hang Hom streets. Originally part of the ancient villages of Toc Tich and Thuong My, which were later combined into the Thuong My village within the old Tho Xuan district, Hang Quat Street has a storied past that dates back to the time of the Le Dynasty. During this period, it was known by its Vietnamese name, but after the French colonial era, it was briefly renamed "rue des Eventails" before reverting to its original Vietnamese name in 1945. Today, it belongs to Hang Gai Ward, Hoan Kiem District.

The street's name reflects its historical significance as a center for quill production and trade. The craft of making fans here was brought by residents from Dao Xa Village, also known colloquially as Dong Quat, in An Thi District of Hung Yen Province. These settlers established themselves in Hang Quat Street, where they not only produced fans but also worshipped their patron saint, a Mr. Dao, at a local temple now located at number 4 on the street, known as the Spring Fair Temple or "cho quat mua xuan." This temple served as a marketplace for various types of fans during the spring season, attracting buyers from different provinces.

Fans made in Hang Quat Street are diverse and include several varieties. Quat Lu, crafted in Kim Lu Village of Thanh Tri District, are made from paper or thin silk-like material. Quat Hoi, originating from Hai Yen Village in Tien Lu District of Hung Yen Province, feature bamboo ribs. Quat Vac, from Canh Hao Village in Thanh Oai District of Hanoi, are distinguished by their intricate bamboo joints that shimmer when held up to light, resembling silk fans. Additionally, there are fans from Vo Village in Nong Vu Commune of Gia Lam District, featuring trellis designs like leaf-shaped or trapezoidal patterns, and quail feather fans from Dong Ding Village in Ha Dong District.

The tradition of fan-making in Hanoi is deeply rooted in history, as noted by Nguyen Trai in his work "Du Dia Chi," where he mentions that the left ward of Thang Jing, now known as Hanoi, had a fan-making industry. Although the exact location of this ward is no longer certain, it is known that until the late 19th century, there was still a fan-making industry in the area around the current Huu Street. However, Hang Quat Street remains the most renowned for its fan-making, particularly in Thuong My Village, which still retains its temple and shrine at numbers 64 and 74 respectively.

Visitors to Hang Quat Street can immerse themselves in the rich cultural heritage of fan-making, witnessing the intricate craftsmanship and the variety of materials used in creating these traditional fans. The atmosphere is one of nostalgia and tradition, with the sounds of bamboo being cut and shaped, and the sight of artisans meticulously assembling each fan. The street itself is lined with shops that not only produce fans but also sell them, offering a range of styles and designs that reflect the skill and creativity of the local artisans. The experience of walking through Hang Quat Street is a journey back in time, allowing visitors to appreciate the enduring legacy of this traditional craft and the cultural significance it holds in the heart of Hanoi.

Hang Quat Street, a historic thoroughfare nestled within the heart of old Thang Long, now known as modern-day Hanoi, is a testament to the rich tapestry of Vietnamese craftsmanship and cultural heritage. The street was originally built upon the ancient lands of two villages, Toc Tich and Thuong My, which were part of the former district of Tien Tuc, later renamed Thuong My, in the old county of Tho Xuan. This area has been a hub for artisans and craftsmen since its inception, with the establishment of a distinct community dedicated to the craft of fan-making, brought over by settlers from Dao Xa village, also known as Dong Quat, in An Thi district of Hung Yen province.

The tradition of fan-making in Hang Quat Street began when a group of settlers from Dao Xa village migrated to this area, seeking new opportunities and establishing themselves as skilled artisans. They founded a communal temple, the Spring Fair Temple, located at number 4 on the street, to honor their patron saint and the origins of their craft. This temple, known as the Spring Fair Temple or "Xuan Phiến Thị," symbolizes the seasonal nature of the fan trade, with its peak activity during the spring months.

The fans sold on Hang Quat Street were sourced from various regions, each contributing unique styles and materials. For instance, the Lu fans, crafted in Kim Lu village of Thanh Tri district in Hanoi, featured intricate designs made from paper, thin silk, and even bone and ivory. Similarly, the Hoi fans from Hai Yen village in the district of Tien Lu, Hung Yen, were renowned for their bamboo ribs, while the Vac fans from Canh Hoach village in Thanh Oai district of Hanoi were distinguished by their meticulous assembly, appearing almost like silk fans when unfurled. Additionally, fans from Dong Lac village in Dong Nac district of Tu Liem, Hanoi, showcased a variety of shapes such as willow leaves and trapezoids, while those from Vo village in Gia Lam district of Hanoi were known for their rice grain patterns. The street also boasted fans adorned with goose feathers, crafted in Dong Ding village of Ha Dong district in Hanoi.

Over time, Hang Quat Street evolved beyond its original purpose of selling fans and musical instruments. Today, the shops along the street have diversified into other trades, offering a wide array of goods including large and small altars, embroidered paintings, calligraphy, and ceremonial decorations used in funeral and wedding ceremonies. These items reflect the deep-rooted traditions and cultural practices that continue to thrive in the area.

Historically, Hang Quat Street comprised three distinct sections: Hang Quat, Hang Dan, and Ma Vi. The section known as Hang Dan was particularly famous for its music shops, where various types of traditional musical instruments such as the moon lute, zither, monochord, and flute were crafted and sold. By the early 20th century, many of these shops had shifted towards producing ornate wooden carvings, including dragon heads, eight treasures, and other decorative elements used in temples and palaces. Later, some of these establishments transitioned again, focusing on more common furniture items like tables, chairs, cabinets, and altar tables.

The segment called Ma Vi was named for its association with the production and sale of theatrical costumes, embroidery, and stage props. The term Ma Vi translates to "horse tail," referring to the use of horsehair in creating flags, banners, and other decorative items used in traditional performances and festivals. This part of the street was a vibrant center for artisans who specialized in crafting elaborate costumes and accessories for traditional theater and folk performances.

Today, visitors to Hang Quat Street can explore a range of historical sites and cultural landmarks. One notable site is the Dau Temple, also known as Thuong My Temple, located at number 64. This temple was named after the extensive mulberry groves that once thrived in the area, providing a livelihood for the local inhabitants. Another significant landmark is the communal house at number 74, which honors the local guardian deity, the Village God.

Educational institutions also dot the landscape of Hang Quat Street, with the Nguyen Van To School, situated at number 43, being one of the oldest private schools in Hanoi. Originally established as the Tri Tri School, it has played a crucial role in the educational development of the region.

Among the notable figures associated with Hang Quat Street is the prominent businessman and artist Nghiêm Xuân Huyến, whose residence at number 21 is a point of interest. He was not only a successful entrepreneur, owning the Rang Dong printing house, but also a journalist and publisher, founding newspapers such as Bac Ky The Thao in 1930 and Con Ong in 1936, which served as platforms for satire and criticism against colonial rule and collaborators. His contributions extended beyond business and journalism, as he was a mentor and father-in-law to the renowned composer Van Cao.

Hang Quat Street remains a living museum of Vietnamese culture, where visitors can immerse themselves in the rich history and traditions of the area, experiencing firsthand the legacy of craftsmanship and cultural significance that has defined this historic street for generations.
