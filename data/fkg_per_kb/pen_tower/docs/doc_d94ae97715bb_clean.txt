The Pen Tower, located in Hoan Kiem Lake, is a five-tiered stone tower standing at 28 meters tall, constructed in the year 1865 during the reign of Emperor Tu Duc, on the site of the former Mount Ducton according to the idea of scholar Nguyen Van Sieu. It stands just outside the entrance to the Thê Húc Bridge, near the Ngoc Son Temple. The tower is built on a rocky hill with a diameter of 12 meters and a height of 4 meters. Its square structure consists of five tiers, each adorned with intricate details. Atop the tower is an inverted quill pen, symbolizing the peak of literary achievement. The pen itself, both stem and nib, rises to a height of 0.9 meters. On the middle three tiers, inscribed vertically, are the characters "Tá Thanh Thiên" (寫青天), which translates to "Write upon the blue sky."

The complex of the Pen Tower represents not only literary excellence but also commemorates the martial prowess of the Lord Trinh in suppressing the uprising led by Nguyen Danh Phuong. The tower is erected on a mound filled with rock fragments, symbolizing a mountain named Ducton. In his essay "Bút Tháp chí," written in 1865 and engraved on the tower, Nguyen Van Sieu describes that while constructing Mount Ducton on the eastern shore of the lake, the Lord Trinh also built Mount Jade Pebble on the western shore. These two mountains were distinct features of Thang Long (modern-day Hanoi). Adjacent to Mount Ducton was the Khang Thuy Palace, which was later destroyed during the reign of Le Chieu Tong, though its name remained in two villages called Left Khang Thuy and Right Khang Thuy. By the Nguyen dynasty, these two villages merged into one called Bao Khanh Village, now known as Bao Khanh Street, a historical relic.

At the beginning of the Thê Húc Bridge lies the Inkstone Pavilion, where an inkstone carved from green stone in the shape of half a peach is placed atop the first gate leading into the temple. This inkstone measures 0.97 meters in length, 0.8 meters in width, and 0.3 meters in height, with a circumference of 2 meters. Three toads support the inkstone, resembling three legs of a tripod. Engraved on the inkstone is a brief inscription by Nguyen Van Sieu, consisting of 64 Chinese characters, whose meaning is condensed yet profound. A song titled "Nghiên Bút Non Sông" (Inkstone and Pen of Mountains and Rivers) was composed by Ding Yang, celebrating the Pen Tower.
