Hang Bong Street, located in Hoan Kiem District of Hanoi, is a vibrant thoroughfare that stretches for 932 meters and spans a width of 9 meters. The street intersects with several other historic streets such as Hang Trong, Hang Hom, Hang Gai, Hang Manh, Ly Quoc Su, Duong Thanh, Phu Doan, Hang Da, and Quan Su, ultimately leading to Ngang Cua Nam. This bustling area was formerly known as "rue du Coton" during the French colonial period, and after the revolution, it retained its name as Hang Bong Street.

The history of Hang Bong Street is deeply rooted in the traditional crafts and trades of old Hanoi. Originally, the street was divided into several segments, each with its own distinct name and purpose. The initial section, from where it meets Hang Gai Street to the end of Hang Manh Street, was called Hang Hai, named after the production and sale of wooden clogs adorned with silk embroidery. These clogs were made with square wooden soles and embroidered silk tips, while imitation clogs were crafted from colorful paper and used for religious ceremonies. This part of the street belonged to the Kim Bat Thượng village, which was part of the Tien Tuc district.

Following Hang Hai, the next segment was known as Hang Bong Dem, extending from Hang Manh to Hang Da. This stretch was dedicated to the production and sale of cotton quilts and mattresses, reflecting the activities of the Kim Bat Ha village, also within the Tien Tuc district. Both these villages were part of the Thuong My district until the mid-19th century when they merged with the Co Vu Thượng village to form the Kim Co village.

Further along, the street continued with the Hang Bong Cua Quyen section, which featured a large banyan tree known as the Co Quyen tree. After a lightning strike destroyed the shrine near this tree, the banyan was felled, but the name persisted, becoming synonymous with the area. This segment belonged to the Thuong Mon Dong Ha village, part of the Tien Nghiem district, which later became the Vinh Xuong district.

The final stretch of Hang Bong Street, known as Hang Bong Lo, extended from the Hoa Vu alley to the Cua Nam junction. This area specialized in dyeing blue cloth, earning it the name Hang Lam. It was situated in the Yen Trung Ha village, also within the Tien Nghiem district.

Throughout Hang Bong Street, one can find numerous ancient temples and communal houses that reflect the rich cultural heritage of the area. Notable among these are the Phuc Hau Temple, Tam Thanh Communal House, Luong Ngoc Temple, Kim Hu Communal House, Vong Tien Shrine, Thien Tien Communal House, and Dong My Communal House. Each of these structures holds significant historical and spiritual importance.

For instance, the Phuc Hau Temple, now located on the second floor of number 2, honors the ancestor of the mirror-making trade and a guardian who helped locate lost children. The temple's history is intertwined with the legend of Tran Nhu Dinh, a figure associated with the Tran dynasty, who taught the craft of mirror-making to the people of Kim Bat and Co Vu villages. His dedication to helping lost children earned him the title of Phuc Hau, a revered protector of the community.

Another important site is the Luong Ngoc Temple at number 68A, built by residents from the Luong Ngoc village in Hai Hung province who migrated to Thang Long. This temple serves as a place of worship and remembrance for their ancestral homeland.

The Kim Hu Communal House at number 95 is dedicated to Tran Hung Dao, a national hero. The temple displays a pair of poetic couplets that celebrate his military prowess and heroic legacy. Similarly, the Vong Tien Shrine at number 120B commemorates the legendary encounter between King Le Thanh Tong and a fairy. According to folklore, the king met a beautiful maiden at the Jade Lake Pagoda and invited her to ride in his sedan chair. However, she vanished before reaching the Dai Hung Gate. In response, the king ordered the construction of a pavilion at the spot where she disappeared, naming it the Vong Tien Pavilion. Historical records indicate that this shrine was likely constructed after the rebuilding of the Thang Long Citadel in 1805.

The Thien Tien Communal House at number 120 may have been established to honor Ly Thuong Kiet, another significant historical figure. These temples and communal houses not only serve as places of worship but also preserve the cultural and historical essence of Hang Bong Street.

In addition to its religious and historical significance, Hang Bong Street has played a crucial role in the development of modern Hanoi. During the early years of French colonial rule, the first elementary school in the city was established here. The street also saw the emergence of the first Vietnamese-owned printing presses, such as Nghiem Ham Ink Shop, Trung Bac, and Mac Dinh Tu. Moreover, the renowned poet Tan Da operated a bookstore, Tan Da Bookstore, at number 58, contributing to the intellectual and cultural life of the city.

Hang Bong Street is also notable for its connection to the early days of the Communist Party of Vietnam. At number 177, the official establishment of the Hanoi Municipal Party Committee took place in June 1930, with Nguyen Ngoc Vu appointed as the Secretary of the City Committee. This event marked a pivotal moment in the political history of Hanoi and Vietnam.

Today, Hang Bong Street remains a testament to the rich tapestry of Hanoi’s past, blending traditional crafts, historical landmarks, and modern urban life. Visitors can explore the narrow lanes lined with old houses, visit the ancient temples, and immerse themselves in the vibrant atmosphere of this historic street. The street's architecture, characterized by its traditional Vietnamese houses with ornate facades and intricate wood carvings, provides a glimpse into the city's architectural heritage.
