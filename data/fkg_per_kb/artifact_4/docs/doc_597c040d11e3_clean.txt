The National Treasures at the Heritage Conservation Center of Thang Long - Hanoi is a repository of hundreds of valuable artifacts with a historical depth spanning thousands of years. Among these treasures, several have been officially recognized as national treasures by the government, each carrying significant historical and cultural importance. One such notable artifact is the set of steps from the Kinh Thien Palace during the early Le dynasty, which was constructed in 1428 under King Le Thai To and completed during the reign of King Le Thanh Tong. This palace served as a venue for solemn ceremonies and was the most important royal building, used for receiving foreign envoys and discussing state affairs.

During the late 19th century, under French colonial rule, the Kinh Thien Pavilion was demolished, but the steps were preserved. The set includes two dragon-carved steps in the center and two cloud-shaped dragon-carved steps on either side, forming three pathways up and down the stairs. The central pathway was reserved exclusively for the king. The dragon carvings on these steps are a distinctive artistic heritage structure, embodying the indomitable spirit of the Vietnamese people through their dynamic posture, with heads raised high. The seven sections of the dragon's body undulate gracefully, reflecting Confucian standards in dragon imagery, while floral motifs like lotus vines and other decorative elements convey Buddhist thoughts. The size, structure, and ornamental designs of the cloud-shaped dragon steps are unique and not found in similar relics or sites.

On December 31, 2020, this set of steps from the Kinh Thien Palace was officially recognized as a National Treasure in the ninth batch by the Prime Minister via Decision No. 2283/QD-TTg. Another significant artifact is the dragon head from the Tran dynasty, a complete round sculpture standing 60 centimeters tall, with its widest point from mouth to back mane measuring 52 centimeters and the width of the mane being 17 centimeters. This dragon head is a crucial component in the decorative collection on traditional architectural roofs, particularly those from the Ly and Tran dynasties. Over time, dragon head decorations on roof structures have evolved in terms of shape, structure, and symbolic meaning.

In the eleventh batch of National Treasures announced by the Government on January 30, 2023, through Decision No. 41/QD-TTG, the Imperial Citadel of Thang Long contributed four out of the 27 national treasures across the country. These include the dragon head from the Tran dynasty, a collection of blue-flowered porcelain bowls and plates used by the imperial family during the early Le dynasty, the steps from the Kinh Thien Palace during the middle Le dynasty, and cannonballs from the middle Le dynasty.

The collection of blue-flowered porcelain bowls and plates used by the imperial family during the early Le dynasty is a unique piece, holding special historical and cultural value linked to the archaeological site of the Imperial Citadel of Thang Long at 18 Hoang Dieu Street. These imperial dining utensils showcase advanced pottery techniques, high firing temperatures, individual firing, intricate and meticulous patterns, reflecting the skill and aesthetic sense of talented potters. Similarly, the cannonballs from the middle Le dynasty represent a type of firearm used in Vietnam since the end of the Tran dynasty around the late 14th century, illustrating the development of cannon technology in Vietnam's history.

Another significant artifact is the steps from the Kinh Thien Palace during the middle Le dynasty, a unique relic associated with the Kinh Thien Palace, one of the most important historical sites within the World Heritage Site of the Imperial Citadel of Thang Long - Hanoi. As part of the Kinh Thien Palace, these steps reflect the historical changes of the palace over time. The dragons carved on these steps move dynamically from top to bottom along the vertical axis of the steps, with intricate and unique decorative patterns that do not repeat in similar relics or sites.

Two imperial porcelain bowls from the early Le dynasty also stand out, with slight differences in size but identical in structure, theme, and decorative techniques. The thinness of the porcelain bone is described as "thin as an eggshell," with high translucency allowing light to pass through, indicating the high level of craftsmanship and production techniques during the early Le dynasty.

The leaf-shaped plaque with phoenixes from the Imperial Citadel of Thang Long is another national treasure recognized by the government in 2021. Among the thousands of ceramic artifacts discovered at the Imperial Citadel of Thang Long, this plaque is considered a representative example, highly valued for its cultural, historical, artistic, and sculptural significance. The plaque consists of two parts, the leaf-like body and the base, with both sides adorned with pairs of phoenixes. It is a completely handcrafted work of art, showcasing the refinement and talent of artisans from the Ly dynasty in the 11th and 12th centuries.

At the beginning of 2023, the statue of King An Duong Vuong was approved for recognition as a national treasure. This statue, cast using the lost-wax method with bronze alloy, depicts the king seated on a pedestal, his hands holding a sword, exuding a dignified and majestic demeanor. He wears a two-tiered crown decorated with "two dragons facing the sun," and his square face reflects the style of portrait sculpture. His attire includes a long robe with wide sleeves reaching to his shoes, adorned with a broad jade belt hanging loosely, and he wears curved-toe shoes.

This statue is the only known representation of King An Duong Vuong, making it a unique and invaluable historical, cultural, and scientific artifact of the nation. The Heritage Conservation Center of Thang Long - Hanoi continues to organize various activities, including academic seminars and memorial ceremonies, to promote the recognition and appreciation of these national treasures. Through these efforts, visitors can immerse themselves in the rich tapestry of Vietnamese history and culture, experiencing firsthand the grandeur and sophistication of past dynasties.

The Artifact at the heart of the ancient site of Hoang Thanh Thang Long, specifically within the Hoang Thanh Thang Long relic, is a remarkable testament to the architectural and artistic prowess of the Vietnamese dynasties. The primary focus lies on the remnants of the Kinh Thien Palace, where two dragon-thresholds stand majestically, one at the front and another at the rear. The southern threshold, facing the sun, comprises nine steps, each approximately 20 centimeters high and 40 centimeters wide, stretching 13.6 meters from east to west. From the dragon's head to the wall, the length measures 5.45 meters. This threshold is divided into three pathways, with the central path distinguished by a pair of dragon carvings crafted during the reign of King Le Thanh Tong in 1467. These dragons are beautifully sculpted, embodying a robust and healthy form with their heads raised high above the first step, their bodies gracefully curving over the nine steps. Their large heads feature open mouths clutching jewels, prominent noses, bulging eyes, and whiskers and hair styled into saber-like shapes that flow backward. The dragon's ears resemble those of a buffalo, while its horns extend straight back, nearly touching the body. Along the spine, there are large, rounded scales carved into the stone, adding depth and texture to the sculpture.

On either side of the threshold, there are decorative railings shaped like stylized dragons, known as cloud-transformed dragons. The inner side of these railings is adorned with cloud patterns, while the outer side showcases intricate designs of trailing chrysanthemum flowers. These motifs cover the entire surface of the stylized dragon carvings, enhancing their aesthetic appeal. Outside the threshold, two clouds transformed into dragons flank the structure, adding a sense of dynamism and movement. These massive stone blocks measure 36 to 39 centimeters thick, with the heads being slightly thicker. They feature continuous undulating lines and protruding ridges running along their lengths, creating a visually striking effect.

The northern threshold, positioned behind the palace, consists of seven steps, reflecting a more modest scale compared to the southern counterpart. It also features a pair of dragon carvings, dating back to the mid-to-late Le dynasty period (17th-18th centuries). These dragons are depicted in a round, solid form, extending 3.4 meters in length. The railings on both sides of this threshold are decorated with lotus flowers, waves, flames, cloud patterns, and dragon-transformed fish, further enriching the visual experience.
