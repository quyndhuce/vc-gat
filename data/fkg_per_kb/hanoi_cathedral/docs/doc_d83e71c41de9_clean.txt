St. Joseph's Cathedral, also known as the Grand Cathedral of Hanoi or House of Worship of Saint Joseph, stands majestically on Nhà Chung Street in the Hoàn Kiếm District of Hanoi, Vietnam. This iconic structure, a testament to the Gothic Revival architectural style, was constructed in the late 19th century and now serves as the cathedral of the Roman Catholic Archdiocese of Hanoi. Named after Joseph, the patron saint of Vietnam, the cathedral is a blend of historical significance and spiritual devotion.

Construction of the cathedral began in 1884, inspired by the architectural grandeur of Notre Dame de Paris. It was completed in December 1886, just a year before the establishment of French Indochina as a colonial territory. The church was one of the earliest buildings erected by the colonial government of French Indochina, marking the beginning of extensive French influence in the region. The cathedral's construction was facilitated by the French missionary and apostolic vicar of West Tonkin, Paul-François Puginier, who secured permission from the colonial administration.

The cathedral's location is significant, situated west of Hoàn Kiếm Lake in the heart of the Old Quarter. It is positioned at the intersection of Nhà Thờ Street and Nhà Chung Street, surrounded by upscale boutiques, silk markets, and restaurants, making it a popular destination for both tourists and locals. The cathedral's main entrance is typically open only during Mass, but visitors can access the premises through a side door in the compound wall of the diocese, where they must ring a bell to enter the cathedral itself.

Historically, the site of the cathedral was once occupied by the Báo Thiên Pagoda, which had been abandoned since its collapse in 1542. The ruins of the pagoda were cleared to make way for the new cathedral, which was consecrated on December 24, 1886. The cathedral's construction marked a pivotal moment in the colonial history of Hanoi, following the capture of the Hanoi Citadel by French forces in 1873 and the subsequent establishment of French control over the city.

During the period of communist rule in North Vietnam after 1954, the Catholic Church faced severe persecution. Many priests were arrested, and church properties were confiscated. However, St. Joseph's Cathedral was eventually reopened for Mass on Christmas Eve in 1990, marking a turning point in the relationship between the Vietnamese government and the Catholic community.

The cathedral's architecture is a striking example of Neo-Gothic design, featuring twin bell towers that rise to a height of 103 feet (31 meters). These towers, adorned with five bells each, bear a resemblance to those of Notre Dame de Paris, reflecting the architects' intention to emulate the Parisian cathedral. The exterior walls are constructed of granite stone slabs and brick, with concrete facings. Over the years, the cathedral's exterior has deteriorated due to pollution, necessitating a major renovation between July 2020 and May 2022 to restore its original beauty and structural integrity.

Inside, the cathedral's interior is equally impressive, with tall stained glass windows and pointed arches. The stained glass was crafted in France and shipped to Vietnam. The ceiling features rib-vaulting reminiscent of medieval European architecture. The nave is weathered, while the sanctuary is adorned with polished gilt-trimmed wood, similar to the style found in Phát Diệm Cathedral. A statue of the Virgin Mary is housed in a palanquin, a local custom, placed to the left of the nave.
