In the heart of a bustling city, tucked away in a quiet alley off Phạm Huy Thông street in Gò Vấp district, lies a unique sanctuary dedicated to the preservation of ethnic instruments. This hidden gem is the home of Mr. Đức Dậu, a passionate collector and performer of traditional Vietnamese musical instruments who has spent over three decades amassing a vast collection of more than 2,000 artifacts. His dedication to these ancient instruments is not just a hobby but a profound commitment to preserving the rich cultural heritage of Vietnam's 54 ethnic groups.

The journey of Mr. Dậu with ethnic instruments began in his childhood when he lived in Hà Nội on Phố Huế. Across from his house was a theater where he was exposed to the vibrant world of music and arts, which sparked his lifelong passion for traditional sounds. Despite initial resistance from his family, who wanted him to pursue medicine, Mr. Dậu’s destiny was intertwined with music. A pivotal moment came during a funeral procession in Ba Vì, Sơn Tây, where a monk advised his father that Mr. Dậu was destined to be an artist rather than a doctor. This divine guidance set the course for his life’s work.

In the 1970s, Mr. Dậu joined various performing arts troupes, honing his skills while supporting himself financially. By 1983, he secured a position at the Institute of Music and Dance in Hà Nội, where he began his formal study and collection of ethnic instruments. This period allowed him to learn from experts in Vietnamese culture and music, enriching his understanding and appreciation of these instruments. In 1986, he moved to Sài Gòn with his family, further expanding his reach into the diverse musical traditions of southern Vietnam.

Mr. Dậu’s collection is a testament to his tireless efforts and deep respect for the communities that create and preserve these instruments. Each piece in his collection carries a story, a history, and a cultural significance that goes beyond mere material value. To acquire these instruments, he often had to spend years building relationships with local communities, learning their customs, and earning their trust. For instance, obtaining a particular instrument might require several visits over four years, during which he would learn how to play it and understand its importance within the community. Only then could he hope to secure the right to keep it in his collection.

One of the most remarkable pieces in his collection is a 300-year-old H’gơr drum from the Tây Nguyên region. This drum, used in rituals celebrating the first month of a child’s life or birthdays, is crafted from wood and covered with buffalo, elephant, and rattan skin. The drum’s longevity is attributed to a special preservation method known only to the Ede people, involving a bitter leaf solution that repels insects. This method ensures that the drum remains intact despite the passage of centuries.

The house itself is a reflection of Mr. Dậu’s commitment to maintaining the authenticity of these instruments. Built as a wooden structure, it contrasts sharply with the concrete buildings around it, creating a space that resonates with the natural sounds of ethnic instruments. Inside, the walls are adorned with rows of drums stacked high and shelves filled with flutes and percussion instruments. The layout is designed to facilitate easy access and performance, making it more than just a museum but a living repository of traditional music.

Visitors to Mr. Dậu’s home can experience a unique blend of visual and auditory delights. The instruments are displayed in a way that highlights their intricate craftsmanship and historical significance. For example, the Chapi flute, a 13-stringed instrument from the regions of Kon Tum and Gia Lai, is showcased alongside other instruments, each telling a story of its origin and cultural importance. Mr. Dậu often performs for visitors, demonstrating the soulful melodies and rhythms that these instruments produce. One such performance includes the song "Đôi Chân Trần," a composition reflecting the resilience and struggles of the Tây Nguyên people. The combination of the bright, lively tones of the Chapi flute and Mr. Dậu’s powerful vocals creates a captivating experience that transports listeners to the heart of ethnic Vietnam.

Beyond the physical collection, Mr. Dậu’s work extends to education and cultural promotion. He has authored a book titled "Tiếng Vọng Ngàn Năm" (Echoes Through the Ages), which serves as a valuable resource for researchers and educators interested in traditional Vietnamese music. Additionally, he founded the Phù Đổng Percussion Ensemble in the 1980s, providing a platform for showcasing the instruments he has collected. Through performances and educational initiatives, Mr. Dậu aims to foster a greater appreciation for the cultural significance of ethnic instruments among the broader public.

Visiting Mr. Dậu’s home is not merely an exploration of artifacts but an immersive experience into the rich tapestry of Vietnamese musical traditions. Each instrument tells a story of cultural continuity, craftsmanship, and the enduring spirit of the communities that have preserved them. From the intricate carvings on wooden flutes to the rhythmic beats of ancient drums, every element contributes to a deeper understanding of the cultural heritage embedded in these instruments.
