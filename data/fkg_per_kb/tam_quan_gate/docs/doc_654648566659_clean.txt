The Tam Quan Gate is a traditional architectural feature commonly found at temples and pagodas in Vietnam, as well as some palaces and communal houses. An example of this can be seen at the Co Mat Institute in Hue.

The Tam Quan Gate embodies Buddhist concepts, representing the three perspectives of Buddhism: "Yeu quan," "Khong quan," and "Trung quan." These symbolize the illusory nature, the impermanent void, and the middle way between both extremes. Another interpretation suggests that the gate represents the entrance to the Three Jewels of Buddhism. Some theories propose that the gate symbolizes the "three gates of liberation" in Zen Buddhism, namely the gate of Emptiness, the gate of Non-action, and the gate of Formlessness. Consequently, countries where Zen Buddhism is not practiced do not construct Tam Quan Gates as temple entrances.

Architecturally, the Tam Quan Gate typically consists of three passageways, with the central passage being larger than the two side passages. The walls of the gate can be made of wood, brick, or stone, and the structure is usually roofed. On either side of the passageways, there are often decorative couplets, and the top of the gate may bear the name of the temple or the gate itself.

When the gate is designed on a larger scale, it may have two tiers or even a third tier, especially if constructed from brick or stone. In such cases, the upper part often serves as a bell tower for hanging bells, gongs, and drums used in temple rituals.

There is also a variant known as the "four-pillar" style Tam Quan Gate, which uses four columns to divide the space into three pathways, with the central columns being taller than the side ones. Above these columns, there is an ornamental beam forming the gateway's lintel. A notable example of this style is the Tam Quan Gate at Chua Lang, which features curved roofs, giving it a unique appearance in Vietnamese traditional architecture.

In some instances, the Tam Quan Gate has been modified to include five pathways, as seen in the case of Chua Set in Hanoi.

Although primarily a Buddhist architectural element, the Tam Quan Gate has also been adopted by other religious structures such as shrines, communal houses, Taoist temples, and Cao Dai churches.

In Cao Dai religion, most religious buildings incorporate Tam Quan Gates or variations thereof. Notably, the Inner Compound of the Holy See in Tay Ninh features twelve Tam Quan Gates built in three different architectural styles. The largest of these is called the Main Gate, while the eleven smaller ones are numbered from one to twelve, excluding number five. The Main Gate resembles a traditional Tam Quan Gate but its arched roof design gives it a resemblance to three separate Torii gates rather than a single Tam Quan Gate.
