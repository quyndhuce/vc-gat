Tran Quoc Pagoda, also known as Chua Trấn Quốc or Trấn Quốc Tự, is the oldest Buddhist temple in Hanoi, Vietnam. Originally built in the sixth century during the reign of Emperor Lý Nam Đế, the pagoda has a history spanning over 1,500 years. Initially named Khai Quốc (National Founding) and situated on the banks of the Red River, the temple faced challenges due to the river's encroachment. In 1615, it was moved to Kim Ngư (Golden Fish) islet in West Lake, where it remains today, connected to the mainland by a small causeway. The last major renovation took place in 1815, when the main sanctuary, reception hall, and posterior hall of the dead were restored.

The pagoda is part of the larger Trấn Quốc Temple complex and houses the ashes of significant monks. It is home to monks who have resided there for centuries, teaching Buddhism to the public. Before visitors arrive, the monks pray at various shrines throughout the grounds. Monks at Tran Quoc Pagoda adhere to a celibate lifestyle and do not live with families.

Throughout its history, the temple has been known by different names, including An Quốc (Pacification of the Realm) and Trấn Bắc (Guardian of the North). The site is rich in Buddhist symbolism, featuring the eight-spoked wheel, which represents the Noble Eightfold Path, and numerous lotus flower statues. These lotuses symbolize purity of the mind, body, and speech, as well as enlightenment and achievement. Carvings of lotus flowers decorate the stone walls, reflecting the natural beauty of Vietnam.

The pagoda's harmonious architecture, complemented by its waterfront setting, makes it a picturesque attraction. Notable among its historical artifacts are statuary pieces dating back to 1639. Additionally, the grounds include a Bodhi tree, a cutting from the original tree in Bodh Gaya, India, under which the Buddha attained enlightenment. This tree was gifted in 1959 during the visit of Indian President Rajendra Prasad.

Visitors to the main shrine are given incense to burn as an offering. They also make offerings of food or small amounts of money to seek good fortune. Incense is presented in odd numbers, as these are considered lucky in Chinese and Vietnamese cultures, while even numbers, particularly the number 4, are seen as unlucky. Offerings are typically vegetarian, respecting the dietary restrictions of the resident monks.

The temple worships goddesses known as "Mẫu," or Mothers, whose shrines are located in the courtyard. These include the green Mother, who oversees mountains and forests; the white Mother, who governs water; and the red Mother, who rules the sky. These deities are among the oldest worshipped in Vietnam, predating Buddhism.

Worshipers can visit the temple at any time, but it is especially popular during the first month of the Vietnamese calendar. They pray at numerous small and large shrines scattered throughout the temple grounds.
