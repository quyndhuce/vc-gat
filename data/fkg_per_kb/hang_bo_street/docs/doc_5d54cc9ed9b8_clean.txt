Hang Bo Street, located in Hoan Kiem District of Hanoi, is a historic thoroughfare that stretches for 272 meters and is approximately 6 meters wide. It runs from the intersection of Hang Dao and Hang Ngang streets to the junction of Hang The, Thuoc Bac, and Bat Dan streets, intersecting with Hang Can and Luong Van Can streets. This street was originally part of the land belonging to the villages of Xuan Hoa (the eastern segment) and Nhan Noi (the western segment), which were both part of the former Tien Tuc district of Tho Xuan county. By the mid-19th century, the village of Xuan Hoa merged with the village of Yen Hoa to form the village of Xuan Yen, and the district of Tien Tuc was renamed Thuong My.

During the French colonial period, this street was known as "rue des Paniers," or "Basket Street." After the revolution, the official name was retained as Hang Bo Street. Today, it falls within the jurisdiction of Hang Dao and Hang Bo wards in Hoan Kiem District.

The remnants of these ancient villages can still be seen today through the surviving temples and shrines scattered throughout the area. For instance, the Xuan Yen Temple at number 44 Hang Can Street honors Princess Lan Ngoc, while another Xuan Yen Temple at number 6 Luong Van Can Street venerates the original Princess Lan Ngoc. Additionally, the Nhan Noi Temple at number 84A Hang Bo Street also pays homage to Princess Lan Ngoc, and the Nhan Noi Communal House at number 33 Bat Dan Street is dedicated to the White Horse deity. The village of Nhan Noi was formerly known as Hang Nui, suggesting that the area once had a thriving pottery industry, producing clay pots and utensils.

The name "Hang Bo" itself is derived from the middle section of the street, where until the early French colonial era, shops selling various bamboo baskets and other woven goods were concentrated. At the eastern end of the street, near the intersections with Hang Dao and Hang Ngang, the area was historically known as Hang Dep, or "Shoe Street," where vendors sold a variety of wooden clogs and shoes. These included hand-carved wooden clogs, intricately carved wooden sandals with leather straps, and wooden-soled shoes with leather tips, catering to both men and women.

According to genealogies of prominent families, such as those from the Nguyen clan of Du Lam (now part of Dong Anh District), during the late Le dynasty and early Nguyen dynasty (late 18th to early 19th centuries), many officials resided in grand mansions along Hang Bo Street, leading contemporaries to nickname it "O Y Hang," meaning "Black Robe Alley," after a similar alley in the Tang Dynasty capital of China, where high-ranking officials wore black robes.

In the middle of the street, there used to be a communal house built by the Ding Cong silversmith guild to honor their ancestors and trade deities. However, when the guild relocated, the building was sold to a wealthy businessman named Pham Le Bay, who converted it into a three-story commercial building. Today, this structure houses the headquarters of the Labor newspaper.

Around the turn of the 20th century, Hang Bo Street became home to many Chinese immigrants from Chaozhou, who engaged in rice and fruit trading, as well as operating photography studios. The street's rich history and cultural significance continue to attract visitors interested in exploring its traditional crafts, architecture, and the vibrant local life that thrives there.
