The Hanoi Old Quarter, also known as Phố cổ Hà Nội, is the historical civic urban core of Hanoi, Vietnam, situated outside the Imperial Citadel of Thăng Long. Historically, this area served as the residential, manufacturing, and commercial center, with each street specializing in a distinct type of manufacturing or commerce. Another common name for this area is the 36 streets (Hà Nội 36 phố phường), reflecting the original 36 streets or guilds that comprised the urban area of the city.

The official boundaries of the Hanoi Old Quarter were established by a 1995 decision from the Vietnamese Ministry of Construction. The northern boundary is defined by Hàng Đậu street, while the western boundary is set by Phùng Hưng street. To the south, the limits are marked by Hàng Bông street, Hàng Gai street, Cầu Gỗ street, and Hàng Thùng street. In the east, the area is bounded by Trần Quang Khải street and Trần Nhật Duật street. However, several historically significant streets lie outside these official borders due to their preservation of historical character and density of historical streets.

The Hanoi Old Quarter is part of the Hoàn Kiếm District and covers an area of approximately 100 hectares, encompassing 76 streets distributed across 10 wards. Its history dates back to the Lý and Trần dynasties when it was located east of the Imperial Citadel of Thăng Long and along the Red River. Scholar Nguyễn Trãi noted the specialization of certain areas during the Lê dynasty, and the area was fortified with a defensive wall and several gates.

During the Lê dynasty, the area included several lakes and wetlands, notably Thái Cực lake, which were connected to Hoàn Kiếm lake and the Red River. By the late 19th century, these water bodies became isolated. Migration from the surrounding plains and an influx of Chinese traders during the Lê dynasty contributed to the formation of the densest urban area in the city. The French colonial period saw partial rebuilding, including the demolition of two small markets to construct Đồng Xuân Market and the introduction of a tramway. This era also brought French and Indian businessmen to the area for trade.

The Hanoi Old Quarter is renowned for its guild streets, each named after a specific trade, such as Hàng Tre (bamboo wares) and Hàng Đồng (copper wares). While some streets retain their traditional trades, others have shifted to different specialties. For instance, Hàng Buồm (sails street) is now known for Vietnamese cakes and candy. Researchers have documented the cultural evolution of the area during the early 20th century.

Historic sites within the Hanoi Old Quarter include temples, pagodas, and assembly halls. Communal temples, often used for commerce, were common, though many have been lost over time. Notable surviving temples include Mã Mây and Kim Cổ. City gates, except for the Quan Chưởng gate, have largely disappeared. The architectural style of the commercial area features traditional shophouses with slanted tile roofs, predominantly built in the 18th and 19th centuries. Since the late 20th century, the number of traditional houses has declined due to ongoing demolitions and rebuildings.

Preservation efforts have been initiated to restore the deteriorating historic remnants of the Hanoi Old Quarter. In preparation for the 1000th anniversary of Hanoi in 2010, the Hanoi People's Committee allocated 50 billion VND for refurbishing seven key areas.
