The Thai Hoc buildings stand as a significant landmark within the complex of Van Mieu - Quoc Tu Giam, embodying a profound connection between Vietnam's ancient educational traditions and its contemporary cultural identity. This site, originally known as the Thai Hoc Academy during the Le dynasty in the 15th century, was a hub for Nhoist education, housing various structures such as lecture halls, dormitories, and storage facilities for printing blocks. Over time, particularly under the Nguyen dynasty in the 19th century, the site underwent transformations, serving as a school for the Phu Hoai Duc and later being reconstructed to honor the parents of Confucius. However, in 1946, the entire Quoc Tu Giam area suffered extensive damage from French forces.

In 1999, the Thai Hoc buildings were reconstructed to commemorate the 990th anniversary of Thang Long-Hanoi, marking a new chapter in its history while preserving its historical significance. The construction covers an area of 1530 square meters within the larger complex of 6150 square meters, featuring a range of traditional architectural elements. These include the Front Hall (Tien Duong), Rear Hall (Hau Duong), Left Pavilion (Ta Vu), Right Pavilion (Hoa Vu), Bell House, and Drum House, all designed to reflect the traditional architectural style of the original Quoc Tu Giam.

The Front Hall serves as a venue for cultural activities, exhibitions, academic seminars, and memorial ceremonies honoring notable figures. Inside the Rear Hall, visitors can find a statue dedicated to Chu Van An, a revered teacher who served as the headmaster of the Quoc Tu Giam from 1342 to 1370. The hall also houses an exhibition titled "Inspiring the Source of Learning," which highlights the contributions of Chu Van An and other educators to Vietnamese education.

On the second floor of the Rear Hall, there is a shrine dedicated to three emperors who played pivotal roles in establishing the Van Mieu-Quoc Tu Giam and advancing the development of Confucian education in Vietnam: Emperor Ly Thanh Tong (1023-1072), Emperor Ly Nhan Tong (1066-1128), and Emperor Le Thanh Tong (1442-1497). Each emperor is celebrated for their dedication to fostering a culture of learning and respect for teachers and scholars.

Today, the Thai Hoc buildings not only serve as a historical and cultural attraction but also as a living testament to the enduring values of reverence for learning, respect for teachers, and the importance of nurturing talent. Visitors can immerse themselves in the rich tapestry of Vietnamese educational heritage, experiencing the serene atmosphere that echoes the scholarly pursuits of generations past. The meticulous restoration and preservation of these buildings ensure that they remain a vital part of Vietnam’s cultural landscape, offering insights into the country’s deep-rooted traditions and educational legacy.
