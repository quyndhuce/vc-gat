Nestled in the heart of Hanoi, Vietnam, Thap Rua, or Turtle Tower, stands as a diminutive yet profoundly significant architectural marvel on an islet in the serene waters of Hoan Kiem Lake. Situated towards the southern part of the lake, this small tower, though modest in size, commands a central position that imbues it with a timeless charm and historical importance. The tower’s location on Go Rua, or Turtle Mound, is itself steeped in history, having been the site of King Le Thanh Tong’s Fishing Pavilion during the 15th century. Over the centuries, the mound saw various structures, including a communal house built by Lord Trinh in the 17th century, which was later dismantled by the Nguyen Dynasty.

The architectural design of Thap Rua is a fascinating amalgamation of French colonial influence and traditional Vietnamese elements. Constructed in the late 19th century, the tower features a square base with four tiers, each progressively narrower than the one below, culminating in a watchtower at the summit. The lower two floors exhibit Gothic arches characteristic of European architecture, while the curved roof retains the ancient Vietnamese architectural tradition. The tower’s dimensions are meticulously designed, with the lowest floor measuring 6.28 meters in length and 4.54 meters in width, housing ten doors and three bays. Each subsequent floor narrows, with the third floor featuring a single circular door on the eastern facade and a small altar against the western wall, whose origins remain somewhat enigmatic. The topmost floor, a mere 2 meters square, serves as a lookout point, offering panoramic views of the surrounding area.

The tower’s construction is attributed to Nguyen Huu Kim, a local notable who gained prominence during the French colonial era. In 1886, Kim built the three-story tower on the auspicious grounds of Go Rua, earning it the initial name of Thap Ba Ho Kim. The tower’s unique blend of architectural styles—combining the Gothic arches of the lower floors with the traditional Vietnamese curved roof—creates a distinctive aesthetic that resonates with both historical and cultural significance.

Historically, the tower has played a pivotal role in the cultural and political landscape of Hanoi. During the French colonial period, a replica of the Statue of Liberty was installed atop the tower, a symbol that was met with mixed reactions from the local populace. This statue, often derided as the "Dam Xoe Statue," was eventually removed in the 1950s following the establishment of a Vietnamese government led by Prime Minister Tran Trong Kim, marking a significant shift in the tower’s symbolism and identity.

Today, Thap Rua stands as a testament to the complex interplay of colonial and indigenous influences in Vietnamese architecture. Its strategic location in the middle of Hoan Kiem Lake makes it a prominent landmark, drawing visitors and locals alike. The tower’s subdued colors, ranging from moss yellow to old gray, reflect the passage of time and blend seamlessly with the natural surroundings. The calm waters of the lake often mirror the tower’s silhouette, creating a serene and symmetrical composition that enhances its visual appeal. At night, the tower is illuminated by warm yellow lights, casting a gentle glow that transforms it into a beacon of tranquility and historical reverence.

Culturally, Thap Rua is deeply intertwined with the legend of King Le Loi returning the sacred sword to the Golden Turtle, adding layers of mythological significance to its already rich historical tapestry. The tower’s presence in the heart of Hanoi not only serves as a visual focal point but also embodies the city’s soul, a symbol of continuity and resilience.
