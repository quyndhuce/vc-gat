The Huc bridge, known in Vietnamese as Cầu Thê Húc, is a historic footbridge located over Hoàn Kiếm Lake in Hanoi, Vietnam. Commissioned by the scholar Nguyễn Văn Siêu during the reign of Emperor Tự Đức in 1865, the bridge connects the bank of the lake with the Ngọc Sơn Temple. Its name, "Thê Húc," translates to "the rising and perching sun," symbolizing the dawn of new beginnings and hope.

Since its initial construction, the Huc bridge has undergone significant reconstruction twice. The first reconstruction took place in 1897 during the reign of Emperor Thành Thái. The second reconstruction began in 1952 under the leadership of Mayor Thẩm Hoàng Tín after the bridge collapsed due to overcrowding on New Year's Eve. Architect Nguyễn Bá Lăng supervised the rebuilding process, which was completed in 1953. This time, the foundation was reinforced with cement rather than wood, ensuring greater stability and longevity.

The Huc bridge has faced several challenges throughout its history. In 1887, it was the target of an arson attack orchestrated by two young students, Nguyễn Văn Minh and Đức Nghi, who were protesting against French colonial rule. The French had designated the nearby Ngọc Sơn Temple as a residence for one of their officials and banned traditional worship there. In response, the students plotted to burn down the bridge, hoping to drive the French out of the temple and other nearby sites. Their actions succeeded in scaring the French away from the temple, but the plot was eventually uncovered, leading to the arrest and execution of Nguyễn Văn Minh in 1888 at the age of 18.

Photographic evidence of the bridge dates back to the late 19th century, with Charles-Édouard Hocquard capturing images of the bridge in its original form between 1884 and 1885. Today, the Huc bridge stands as a testament to its enduring legacy, consisting of 15 spans supported by 32 round wooden pillars arranged in 16 pairs. The bridge deck is paved, and the railings are painted a deep red, with the characters "Thê Húc Kiều" (栖旭桥) gilded for decoration. While still revered by many, the bridge now attracts a wide array of visitors, both locals and tourists, who pay a small fee to cross it.
