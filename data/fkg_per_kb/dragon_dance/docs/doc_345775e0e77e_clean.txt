The Dragon Dance, a traditional Chinese cultural performance, is often seen during festive celebrations, particularly during Chinese New Year. This dance involves a team of skilled dancers manipulating a long, flexible dragon puppet using poles placed at regular intervals along its length. The dragon, a symbol of China's rich culture, is believed to bring good fortune and luck to the community, with longer dragons signifying greater blessings. Historically, the dragon embodies qualities such as power, dignity, fertility, wisdom, and auspiciousness, and its movements in the dance symbolize these attributes.

The origins of the Dragon Dance can be traced back to the Han dynasty, where ancient texts describe various forms of the dance. During times of drought, rain dances featuring dragon figures were performed, as the dragon was associated with rain in ancient China. For instance, the dragon Yinglong was considered a rain deity, and the Shenlong could control the amount of wind and rain. Dong Zhongshu’s “Luxuriant Dew of the Spring and Autumn Annals” mentions a ritual where clay dragon figures were used in dances to appeal for rain, with the number of dragons, their length, and color varying according to the season. Additionally, during the Han dynasty, dragon dances were part of the popular baixi variety shows, where performers dressed as dragons or other creatures entertained audiences.

Zhang Heng’s “Lyric Essay on Western Capital” describes performances where dancers dressed as green dragons played flutes, and another act where fish transformed into dragons. One such dance, known as "fish-dragon extending," featured a mythical creature transforming from a flounder into a dragon, entertaining foreign guests at the Han court. However, these ancient dances differed significantly from the modern Dragon Dance, which uses lightweight, maneuverable dragons.

References to dragon dances continued into the Tang and Song dynasties. The Song dynasty work “Dreams of Splendor of the Eastern Capital” describes dragon lanterns used during the Lantern Festival, which were constructed from grass and cloth and illuminated with candles. These lanterns were carried and paraded by performers through the streets at night. Over time, various regional dragon dances developed, such as the Fenghua Cloth Dragon from Zhejiang, which dates back to the 1200s, and the Tongliang County dragon dance, which evolved from snake totem worship during the Ming dynasty and gained popularity in the Qing dynasty.

In the modern era, the government of the People's Republic of China promoted traditional folk dances, contributing to the widespread popularity of the contemporary Dragon Dance. Today, the popular form features a smaller, lighter dragon that can be controlled more easily, contrasting with the heavier, longer Southern Dragon designed for processions rather than agile movements. There are many regional variations, including the Zhanjiang dragon dance, where the dragon's body is formed by a human chain of dozens to hundreds of performers, and the Pujiang County dragon dance, where the dragon is made from wooden stools. Over 700 different dragon dances exist across China and Chinese communities worldwide.

Traditionally, retired dragon puppets are burned, but some historical remnants are preserved. The head of the oldest surviving dragon, dating back to 1878 and named Moo Lung, is displayed at the Bok Kai Temple in Marysville, California. Fragments of similarly aged dragons are also kept at the Sweetwater County Historical Museum in Green River, Wyoming.
