Pho, a beloved Vietnamese soup dish, is a staple in Vietnamese cuisine, known for its rich broth, soft rice noodles, fresh herbs, and tender meat, typically beef or occasionally chicken. Originating in the early 20th century in Northern Vietnam, specifically in Nam Định Province, Pho has become a significant part of Vietnamese culinary culture, enjoyed in households, street stalls, and restaurants across the nation. The city of Nam Định is credited with creating the traditional Vietnamese Pho, with the villages of Vân Cù and Dao Cù (or Giao Cù) in Đông Xuân commune being particularly noted for their role in its development.

The history of Pho is somewhat enigmatic, with its exact origins debated. Some believe it evolved independently, while others suggest it shares roots with similar beef noodle soups found in neighboring countries like Thailand and China. The rice noodles and spices used in Pho’s broth reflect Chinese culinary traditions, but the widespread consumption of beef in Vietnam is largely attributed to French colonial influence. During the French colonial era (1887-1954), the introduction of pot-au-feu and the use of beef bones for broth may have influenced the creation of Pho. However, dishes similar to Pho, using water buffalo meat, were already part of the rural cuisine, indicating a pre-existing familiarity with beef-based soups.

According to cultural historian Trịnh Quang Dũng, the modern form of Pho emerged between 1900 and 1907 in Nam Định Province, a region known for its textile market. The availability of beef increased during the French colonial period, leading to the production of beef bones which were used by Chinese workers to create a beef noodle dish similar to Pho. This dish, known as ngưu nhục phấn (or 牛腩粉) in Chinese, gained popularity among workers from Yunnan and Guangdong provinces, eventually spreading to the wider population.

Initially, Pho was sold as a snack by street vendors who carried mobile kitchens on poles called gánh phở. These vendors would shoulder the heavy equipment, including a cauldron over a wood fire and cabinets storing noodles, spices, and cookware. Distinctive felt hats, mũ phở, were worn by these vendors to keep their heads warm. The first fixed Pho stands in Hanoi were established in the early 20th century, with the first "Nam Định style" Pho stand opening in 1925. By the mid-1930s, the number of peddlers declined as stationary eateries became more prevalent.

In the late 1920s, various experiments with ingredients like húng lìu, sesame oil, tofu, and Lethocerus indicus extract were attempted, though these did not gain widespread acceptance. Rare beef slices, phở tái, were introduced by 1930, and chicken Pho appeared in 1939, likely due to beef market closures on certain days. With the partition of Vietnam in 1954, over a million people migrated southward, bringing Pho with them. This migration led to the dish's popularity in Southern Vietnam, where it was less commonly consumed. Variations in meat and broth emerged, along with additional garnishes such as lime, mung bean sprouts, culantro, cinnamon basil, Hoisin sauce, and hot Sriracha sauce. Pho tái began to compete with fully cooked Pho chín in popularity.

In recognition of its cultural significance, Vietnam designated December 12 as the "Day of Pho" in 2017.
