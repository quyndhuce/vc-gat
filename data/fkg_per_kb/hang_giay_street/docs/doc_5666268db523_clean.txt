Hang Giay Street, located in Hoan Kiem District of Hanoi, is a vibrant thoroughfare that stretches for 267 meters and spans a width of 7 meters. This street connects from Hang Chieu Street to Luong Ngoc Quyen Street, intersecting at the corner of Ngoc Gach, Nguyen Sieu, and Hang Bun streets, near the Bạch Mã Temple and Hang Buom Street. The street's history is rich and layered, reflecting its significance in the urban tapestry of Hanoi.

Originally, Hang Giay Street was formed by merging two separate streets: the northern segment, known as Hang Man, extended from Hang Chieu to Ngoc Gach, while the southern part, called Hang Giay, continued from Ngoc Gach to Hai Tượng. These segments were part of the ancient administrative divisions of the area, with the northern section belonging to the village of Co Luong in the Hau Tuc ward, and the southern part to the village of Hai Tuong in the Huu Tuc ward. Both villages were situated on the northern bank of the To Lich River, with the southern side of the river falling under the jurisdiction of Ha Khau ward.

During the French colonial period, this area was designated as Route Number 23 (voie No23). In 1928, the northern part of the street, formerly known as Hang Man, was renamed Rue Lataste, while the southern part, Hang Giay, bore the name of Nguyen Duy Han, a traitor who served as the prefect of Thai Binh province. He was assassinated by Pham Van Trang, a member of the Vietnam Revolutionary League, in Thai Binh city on April 12, 1913. In 1945, the street was renamed Tan Thuat, and in 1949, it finally adopted its current name, Hang Giay. Since then, the street has retained its name through various changes.

Today, Hang Giay Street is part of the Hang Buom ward, maintaining its historical and cultural significance. The street is home to artisans who specialize in shoemaking, a tradition that traces back to the craftsmen from the village of Cham (Truc Lam, Van Lam, Phong Lam) in Tu Ky district of Hai Duong province. These skilled workers established the village of Hai Tuong, where they built a temple dedicated to their craft, located at number 16 in Hai Tuong alley. This temple, originally the Inner Shrine of the Hai Tuong village, now serves as a place of worship for the spirits of the village of Trau Khe, whose residents purchased and renovated the shrine in 1895.

In the heart of Hang Giay Street, at number 30, stands a small temple that was once the Inner Shrine of the Hai Tuong village. The temple's roof still bears the inscription "Trau Khe Inner Shrine," indicating its original purpose. Despite being located on Hang Giay Street, the temple now belongs to the residents of Hang Bac Street, a testament to the complex interweaving of historical and cultural identities in Hanoi.

The atmosphere is filled with the sounds of hammering and the smell of leather, creating a sensory experience that transports one back in time. Alongside the shoemakers, the street also houses a variety of shops selling shoes and other leather goods, offering a glimpse into the rich heritage of Vietnamese shoemaking.
