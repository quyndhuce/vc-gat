Hoan Kiem Lake, also known as Sword Lake or Tả Vọng Lake, is a freshwater lake located in the heart of Hanoi, the capital city of Vietnam. Covering an area of approximately 12 hectares, it is one of the city’s major scenic attractions and a focal point for public life. Historically, the lake has been referred to by different names such as "Lục Thủy Lake," meaning "Green Water Lake," due to the color of its waters, and "Thủy Quân Lake," or "Mariner's Lake."

The lake's current name, Hoan Kiem, translates to "Lake of the Returned Sword" or "Lake of the Restored Sword." This name is derived from a legendary event involving Emperor Lê Lợi. After defeating the Ming Chinese army, Lê Lợi was boating on the lake when a Golden Turtle God (Kim Qui) appeared and requested the return of his magical sword, Heaven's Will. Lê Lợi realized that the turtle was reclaiming the sword that had been given to him by a local deity, the Dragon King (Long Vương), to aid in the battle against the Chinese. Upon completing his mission, Lê Lợi returned the sword to the turtle, renaming the lake to commemorate this significant event. Before this, the lake was known as Tả Vọng, which means "Left Expectation," referring to the time before the Emperor returned the sword.

The lake is home to a legend associated with the giant soft-shell turtles, either of the species Rafetus swinhoei or a distinct species named Rafetus leloi in honor of Emperor Lê Lợi. For many years, these turtles were frequently sighted in the lake, but the last known individual was found deceased in January 19, 2016. Currently, there are only three remaining individuals of the species R. swinhoei.

On the northern shore of Hoan Kiem Lake lies Jade Islet, where the Temple of the Jade Mountain (Ngọc Sơn Temple) stands. Established in the 18th century, the temple honors several notable figures including the 13th-century military leader Trần Hưng Đạo, who successfully repelled Mongol invasions; the classical scholar Văn Xương; and Nguyễn Văn Siêu, a renowned writer and official who undertook renovations of the temple in 1864. The islet is connected to the mainland by the Thê Húc Bridge, a vermilion-red wooden structure whose poetic name translates to "Perch of the Morning Sunlight."
