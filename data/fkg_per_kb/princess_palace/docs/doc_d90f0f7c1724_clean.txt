The Princess Palace, more accurately known as the central area of the Imperial Citadel of Thang Long - Hanoi, is a site that encapsulates over a thousand years of Vietnamese history, culture, and architectural evolution. Spanning an expansive 20 hectares, this historic zone is nestled within the heart of modern-day Hanoi, bordered by four major streets: Phan Dinh Phung to the north, Dien Bien Phu to the south, Nguyen Tri Phuong to the east, and Hoang Dieu to the west. The site is a treasure trove of archaeological findings and historical landmarks, including the ancient North Gate, the Tran Vu Gate, the Rear Tower, dragon stone statues from the Kinh Thien Palace, the Dragon House, the D67 House, and the iconic Flag Tower of Hanoi.

The origins of the Imperial Citadel of Thang Long trace back to the year 1009 when Ly Cong Uan ascended to the throne, marking the beginning of the Ly Dynasty. In July of 1010, he issued an edict to move the capital from Hoa Lu in Ninh Binh to Dai La, which was subsequently renamed Thang Long. The construction of the Imperial Citadel began immediately after the relocation, and by early 1011, it was completed. The citadel was designed according to a tripartite structure known as the "three-layered city," comprising the outermost La City (or Capital City), the middle layer known as the Imperial City, and the innermost layer, the Forbidden City, reserved exclusively for the emperor, his consorts, and a select few courtiers.

Over the centuries, the Imperial City underwent numerous transformations and expansions under various dynasties. During the Tran Dynasty, the city continued to flourish, and subsequent rulers of the Le Dynasty further enhanced its grandeur. However, during the periods of the Mac and Le Dynasties from 1516 to 1788, the Imperial Citadel suffered significant damage due to repeated invasions and conflicts. In 1789, Emperor Quang Trung moved the capital to Phu Xuan, leaving Thang Long as the Northern Capital. Under the Nguyen Dynasty, much of the Imperial City was dismantled to facilitate the construction of a new capital at Phu Xuan, but the Kinh Thien Palace and the Rear Tower were retained as temporary palaces for visiting emperors.

In 1805, Emperor Gia Long ordered the demolition of the old Imperial City walls and replaced them with a smaller fortress following the French Vauban style. This marked a significant shift in the architectural landscape of the city. By 1831, Minh Mang had officially renamed Thang Long as Ha Noi, and the French colonial era saw further modifications to the city's layout. The French dismantled parts of the old fortress to make way for administrative offices and barracks, reflecting their colonial ambitions.

Today, the Princess Palace stands as a testament to the enduring legacy of Thang Long-Hanoi, embodying a living history that spans over a millennium. The archaeological site at 18 Hoang Dieu Street reveals layers of historical significance, with remnants of the ancient city of Dai La from the Tang Dynasty, the palace complexes of the Ly and Tran Dynasties, the central areas of the Dong Cung of the Le Dynasty, and fragments of the provincial capital city of the 19th century. These layers provide a vivid picture of the continuous development and transformation of the imperial city through different eras.

One of the most notable attractions within the Princess Palace is the archaeological site at 18 Hoang Dieu Street. Here, visitors can explore the remains of the ancient city of Dai La, the palace complexes of the Ly and Tran Dynasties, and the Dong Cung of the Le Dynasty. The site offers a unique opportunity to witness the intricate layers of historical architecture and artifacts, providing insights into the urban planning and architectural styles of each period. Among the treasures unearthed are a vast collection of ceramics and pottery used daily in the royal courts throughout the ages, showcasing the advanced craftsmanship and cultural sophistication of the time.

Another highlight is the Flag Tower of Hanoi, constructed in 1812 during the reign of Emperor Gia Long. Standing at an impressive height of 60 meters, the tower consists of a base, a main body, and a watchtower. The base is square-shaped, covering an area of 2007 square meters, and features three tiers that taper upwards. Each tier is adorned with ornamental walls and intricate carvings, reflecting the artistic and architectural prowess of the period. Visitors can climb the tower, ascending through a series of steps that lead to the upper levels, offering panoramic views of the surrounding area.

The Tran Vu Gate, or Tran Vu Gate, is another significant landmark within the Princess Palace. It serves as the entrance to the Kinh Thien Palace and comprises five archways built of stone. Excavations conducted in 2002 revealed ancient pathways and structures dating back to the Tran Dynasty, demonstrating the continuity of architectural elements from earlier periods. The gate provides a glimpse into the historical pathways and the grandeur of the imperial city.

The Kinh Thien Palace itself, though now reduced to its foundation, remains a focal point of the Princess Palace. Located at the center of the complex, it is flanked by the Tran Vu Gate to the front and the Flag Tower to the north. Behind the palace lies the Rear Tower, while the northern and western sides are enclosed by walls with small gates. The palace's ruins evoke a sense of awe and reverence, symbolizing the power and majesty of past empires.

The D67 House, situated within the complex, holds immense historical significance. It served as the headquarters of the General Staff during critical moments in Vietnam's revolutionary history, including the Tet Offensive of 1968, the 1972 offensive, and the decisive campaigns leading to the reunification of Vietnam in 1975. The house stands as a testament to the resilience and strategic importance of the region during these pivotal times.

The Princess Palace, also known as the Hau Lau, Tinh Bac Lau, or Pagode des Dames, is a significant historical structure located in the heart of Hanoi, Vietnam. Originally constructed in 1821 during the Nguyen Dynasty, this palace stands out for its unique blend of religious and cultural significance. Initially, the Princess Palace served as a place of worship dedicated to Buddhism, housing various statues and artifacts that reflect the spiritual life of the region. Over time, it has undergone several transformations, particularly during the late 19th century when it suffered extensive damage and was subsequently rebuilt by the French colonial authorities.

The architecture of the Princess Palace is a testament to the intricate craftsmanship and design sensibilities of the period. The building is primarily constructed from brick, with a lower section that resembles a box-like structure, while the upper part features a series of five interlocking roof tiers. The lowest level of the palace boasts three roof tiers, whereas the upper levels consist of two tiers each. These roofs are covered with tubular tiles, cemented together with mortar, creating a harmonious visual effect. The corners of the building feature curved eaves, adorned with dragon heads at the outer edges and mythical creatures like lions at the inner corners. This elaborate decoration not only adds aesthetic appeal but also reflects the cultural and artistic traditions of the time.

The restoration efforts undertaken by the French have left a lasting impact on the current appearance of the Princess Palace. However, archaeological excavations conducted between 1998 and 1999 revealed a wealth of historical artifacts that provide insight into the site's long and storied past. Among the findings were delicate white porcelain pieces dating back to the early Le dynasty, indicating the presence of imperial goods and suggesting the importance of the location during that era. At a depth of 3.2 meters, archaeologists discovered remnants of a water dock from the early Le dynasty, constructed with bricks and lotus-shaped stone bases characteristic of the Ly and Tran dynasties. These discoveries underscore the central role that the Princess Palace played within the ancient capital of Thang Long during the Ly, Tran, and early Le periods.

Visitors to the Princess Palace today can explore a space imbued with layers of history and cultural significance. The palace's architecture, with its detailed roof structures and ornamental elements, offers a glimpse into the architectural styles and techniques prevalent during different historical epochs. The site itself is surrounded by a tranquil atmosphere, where one can imagine the bustling activity of ancient times, from religious ceremonies to the daily lives of those who once resided in the vicinity. The cultural heritage of the area is further enriched by the nearby historical sites and the vibrant local community, providing visitors with a comprehensive understanding of the region's rich tapestry of traditions and history.
