Nestled along the picturesque shores of West Lake in Hanoi, Tao Sach Pagoda stands as a testament to centuries of spiritual devotion and cultural heritage. This ancient temple, also known as Chùa Tào Sách, is one of the oldest religious sites in the capital city, dating back to the 16th century. Officially named Linh Sơn Temple, it is located at number 386 Lac Long Quan Street in the district of Tay Ho, a serene area known for its tranquil lakeside ambiance. The pagoda's current abbot is the Venerable Thich Nguyen Hạnh, born in 1958, who continues to oversee the spiritual activities and preservation efforts of this revered site.

Tao Sach Pagoda belongs to the Tào Động sect, one of the five major Zen Buddhist schools that have been passed down through generations. Founded near the end of the 16th century by the Zen master Thuy Nguyet, the Tào Động sect has played a significant role in the spiritual life of the region around West Lake. The pagoda not only serves as a place of worship but also houses the activities of the Hoa Nghiêm Association, which uses the teachings of the Flower Adornment Sutra as its guiding principle for conducting rituals aimed at offering blessings to the deceased relatives of its members.

In 2013, the Ling Mountain Pure Land Dharma Assembly was inaugurated at Tao Sach Pagoda, marking another important milestone in the temple’s history. This assembly represents the convergence of Buddhism, Confucianism, and Taoism, a tradition that has been highly esteemed by many emperors throughout Vietnamese history. Regularly, on the first day of each lunar month, the Ling Mountain Dharma Assembly conducts ceremonies to release living creatures into nature, reflecting the deep-rooted belief in compassion and the cycle of life.

Recognized as a national historical and cultural relic since 1993, Tao Sach Pagoda holds immense historical and cultural significance. Inscriptions on steles within the temple grounds provide insight into its origins, with one plaque dating back to 1943 stating that the pagoda was established during the Early Lê Dynasty. Another inscription from the reign of Emperor Bảo Đại further confirms its historical roots, noting that the pagoda was built on the site of an earlier shrine dedicated to the seventh son of King Tran Nhan Tong, Prince Uy Linh Lang.

According to historical records, Prince Uy Linh Lang, a son of King Tran Nhan Tong, lived in what is now the neighborhood of Nhật Tân with his mother, Chiêu Minh Lady. Despite his royal lineage, the prince harbored a deep interest in Buddhism from a young age. However, his request to become a monk was initially denied by his parents. Instead, he constructed a small house by the shores of West Lake where he could study, practice martial arts, and compose poetry with his brothers. After the death of his mother, the king ordered the construction of a memorial hall in her honor, known as the "Chiêu Thánh Shrine."

During the Mongol invasions of Vietnam in 1285, Prince Uy Linh Lang requested permission from his father to join the battle against the invaders. He distinguished himself in several battles and was later honored with the title of Dâm Đàm Great King. Upon his death in 1300, the people of the region brought his memorial tablet to the Chiêu Thánh Shrine, where he was worshipped alongside six other companions. On the site where the prince once studied, a small thatched hut was erected to commemorate him, eventually evolving into the Tao Sach Pagoda we see today.

The architecture of Tao Sach Pagoda reflects its long and storied history. Following a devastating fire in early 2011, the entire complex underwent extensive restoration. Today, the pagoda encompasses various structures such as the main gate, the three-story main hall, the monks’ quarters, the bell tower, dining halls, and shrines dedicated to various deities. The main entrance, or Tam Quan gate, is a two-tiered structure with eight roofs, facing south towards West Lake. Beyond the gate lies a spacious courtyard leading directly to the main hall, or Tam Bảo, which is designed in the traditional chuoi vo style, resembling the shape of a sword hilt.

Inside the main hall, visitors can find numerous statues of deities, including those of the Diamond Warrior, Nine Dragons, Buddha Shakyamuni, the Heavenly King, and the Bodhisattva Guanyin. The upper levels house images of Amitabha Buddha and the Three World Buddhas, creating a serene and reverent atmosphere. To the left of the main hall stands a magnificent statue of the Bodhisattva Avalokitesvara carved from white marble, symbolizing compassion and mercy. This statue, standing tall on a lotus pedestal, is a focal point for devotees seeking spiritual solace and guidance.

The rear of the main hall leads to a small commemorative pavilion, featuring a stele inscribed with the year 1927 during the reign of Emperor Bảo Đại. Nearby, there are shrines dedicated to the Mother Goddesses and the Five Virtuous Elders, housing intricate statues that reflect the rich cultural tapestry of the region. Throughout the pagoda, one can find valuable artifacts such as 42 pairs of couplets, 23 large calligraphy pieces, two ancient bells, and 29 stone steles dating from 1889 to 1945. Among these treasures are several Buddha statues from the 19th century and three images of the Three World Buddhas from the 18th century, each piece a testament to the enduring legacy of Tao Sach Pagoda.
