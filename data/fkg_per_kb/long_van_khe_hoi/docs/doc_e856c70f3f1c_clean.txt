The breathtaking masterpiece known as "Long Van Khe Hoi" adorns the ceiling of the old main hall of the Diệu Đế Temple, a structure that has been preserved with meticulous care despite the passage of time. This stunning artwork was once at the center of heated debates regarding whether to retain the original main hall or demolish it to make way for a new one. The decision to preserve the hall was ultimately made by the temple authorities, who recognized the irreplaceable value of this unique piece of art. To ensure its survival, they engaged the services of the renowned lantern mover Nguyen Van Cu from Ho Chi Minh City to carefully relocate the entire main hall approximately 20 meters backwards. This daring move was completed successfully in late 2022, ensuring that the magnificent painting remained intact.

The relocation process was a testament to the ingenuity and dedication of those involved. The old main hall, weighing over a thousand tons, was moved with precision and care, maintaining the integrity of the intricate dragon mural. After the successful relocation, the hall was refurbished and reinforced to ensure its stability and longevity. Inside, the space now houses bookshelves filled with religious texts, serving as a place of study and worship for the monks of the temple.

Visitors to the Diệu Đé Temple today can marvel at the "Long Van Khe Hoi," which spans over ten meters in length and nearly eleven meters in width, covering almost the entirety of the ceiling. The painting depicts five dragons swirling amidst clouds, while four additional dragons coil around the four large columns, embodying a traditional Buddhist legend. The level of detail and craftsmanship displayed in these dragons is truly remarkable, with each one rendered with exquisite skill and beauty. Despite the challenges posed by historical changes and the need for restoration, the temple has managed to preserve this rare artistic treasure.

The origins of the "Long Van Khe Hoi" date back to the period between 1953 and 1955 when it was painted during the reconstruction of the main hall under the leadership of Venerable Diao Hoang, with contributions from Empress Tu Cung, the mother of Emperor Bao Dai. The hall itself was built between 1844 and 1951, reflecting the distinctive architectural style of the Nguyen Dynasty, characterized by its ornate wooden structures typical of Hue's imperial architecture. The temple's history is deeply intertwined with the royal lineage, having been designated as a national temple by the Nguyen Dynasty until 1945.

Today, the Diệu Đé Temple stands as a significant cultural landmark in Hue, a city renowned for its rich Buddhist heritage. It continues to host important religious ceremonies such as the Buddha Bathing Festival and the Buddha Procession during Vesak celebrations. The temple's unique blend of imperial and religious elements makes it a fascinating destination for tourists and scholars alike. Visitors often express amazement at the temple's ability to preserve both the physical structure and the spiritual essence of the "Long Van Khe Hoi."

The temple authorities have taken great care to maintain the mural's condition, even after relocating the main hall. They have reinforced the walls and ensured that the painting remains untouched, preserving its original state. However, the temple leaders recognize the ongoing challenge of conserving such a delicate work of art. They hope to collaborate with experts and like-minded individuals to develop better preservation techniques for the future.

The "Long Van Khe Hoi" is not just a visual spectacle but also a testament to the enduring legacy of Hue's cultural heritage. Its preservation reflects the commitment of the local community to safeguarding their historical and artistic treasures.
