Hang Luoc Street, located in Hoan Kiem District of Hanoi, is a charming thoroughfare that stretches for 264 meters and is 8 meters wide. It connects the bustling streets of Hang Cot and Hang Ma, offering visitors a glimpse into the rich history and vibrant culture of the city. Originally part of the ancient villages of Phu Tu and Vinh Tru, which were once part of Dong Xuan Commune within the old Tho Xuong County, Hang Luoc Street has undergone several name changes over the years, reflecting its evolving identity and significance.

Before 1901, the street was known as Hang Luoc, named after the production and trade of combs, a craft that thrived during the Le and early Nguyen dynasties. However, by the 20th century, this tradition had faded, and the street became known as Cong Cheo - Hang Luoc, a name that reflects the presence of a diagonal bridge that once spanned the To Lich River. This river, which used to flow through the area, played a significant role in the naming of the street, as the city council decided in 1901 to rename it "To Lich River Street" due to its historical importance. The name change aimed to preserve the memory of the river, which was a crucial element in local folklore and songs. In 1945, the street reverted to its original name, Hang Luoc, only to be renamed again in 1949 as To Lich River Street, before finally returning to its historic name in 1951.

The street retains vestiges of its past, notably two ancient communal houses: the Phu Tu communal house at number 19 and the Vinh Tru communal house at number 59. Both structures honor the Four Holy Ladies, a revered group of women associated with a tragic tale from the Tran Dynasty. Legend has it that during the Tran period, fishermen near the Can Hai Gate (now known as the Con Gate in Quynh Luu district of Nghe An province) found the bodies of four women who had drowned themselves in the sea to escape capture by Mongol invaders. These women were believed to be the queen mother of the Song dynasty and her attendants. The locals built shrines to honor these women, known colloquially as the Con Shrine, and later, sailors from the coastal regions of Nghe An and Ha Tinh would carry small altars dedicated to the Four Holy Ladies aboard their boats as a form of protection against storms and rough seas. Over time, these shrines spread throughout Vietnam, particularly along the Red River and the former course of the To Lich River, where numerous shrines dedicated to the Four Holy Ladies can still be found.

Despite its historical association with comb-making, Hang Luoc Street today is perhaps best known for its annual flower market, which takes place during the Tet holiday season, typically from the 23rd day of the twelfth lunar month until New Year's Eve. This tradition began when the nearby Dong Xuan Market replaced the old Cau Dong Market, which had been situated along the banks of the To Lich River. As the flower trade expanded, vendors found that the Dong Xuan Market could not accommodate the growing number of flower sellers, leading them to seek out new locations. By the early 20th century, Hang Luoc Street emerged as a popular spot for flower vendors, especially during the Tet holiday, when the street transforms into a vibrant marketplace filled with an array of flowers such as peach blossoms, apricot blossoms, and various other blooms from nearby areas like Quang Ba and Nhat Tan.

The flower market on Hang Luoc Street has become an integral part of the city’s cultural landscape, drawing visitors from all over Hanoi to admire the colorful displays and purchase fresh flowers for their homes and temples. Even during times of conflict, such as the war against the French colonialists and the American War, the flower market continued to thrive, albeit sometimes in different locations. During the American War, the market briefly moved to Duong Thanh Street but always returned to Hang Luoc Street, where it remains a beloved tradition. Notably, during the Tết holiday in 1947, when Hanoi was under siege, the flower market did not operate, yet the spirit of the festival persisted. Flowers from Nhat Tan and Quang Ba were sent to the front lines, symbolizing the unity between the front and the rear, and serving as a morale booster for the soldiers.

Today, Hang Luoc Street continues to be a hub of activity, especially during the Tet holiday season, when the air is filled with the scent of blooming flowers and the sounds of festive celebrations. Visitors can explore the narrow lanes lined with traditional Vietnamese architecture, visit the ancient communal houses, and immerse themselves in the lively atmosphere of the flower market. The street offers a unique blend of historical charm and contemporary vibrancy, making it a must-visit destination for anyone interested in experiencing the rich cultural heritage of Hanoi.
