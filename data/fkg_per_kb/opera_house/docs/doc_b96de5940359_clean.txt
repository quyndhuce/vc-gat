The Hanoi Opera House is an architectural masterpiece located in Hanoi, Vietnam, serving as a venue for artistic performances. Situated at No. 1A Trang Tien Street, Cua Nam Ward, the Opera House stands on August Revolution Square, not far from Hoan Kiem Lake and the Vietnam History Museum, adjacent to the Hilton Hanoi Opera Hotel, overlooking the Opera House Garden and the August 19th Garden. Construction began under French supervision in 1901 and was completed in 1911, inspired by the design of the Paris Opera Garnier but adapted to a smaller scale and local climate conditions. The architects Harlay and Broyer incorporated elements reminiscent of theaters in southern France, with organizational layouts, performance spaces, staircases, and entrance halls similar to those found in European theaters of the early 20th century. Despite its eclectic architectural style, the Hanoi Opera House retains a strong neoclassical French appearance, particularly in its structural design, black tile roof, and interior decorative motifs. Although it was built later than other opera houses in Ho Chi Minh City and Hai Phong, the Hanoi Opera House boasts the most complete architecture and has become a familiar and distinctive landmark of Hanoi.

Since its completion, the Hanoi Opera House has played a significant role as one of the city's major cultural centers, hosting frequent cultural activities and artistic performances. It served as the birthplace and showcase for Vietnamese theater and stage arts, as well as symphonic, choral, operatic, and ballet performances. Moreover, the Opera House holds historical significance, having been the site where the first National Assembly of independent Vietnam convened and adopted the 1946 Constitution, the country’s first constitution, along with the list of ministers for the Democratic Republic of Vietnam United Front Government (led by Ho Chi Minh), the National Resistance Committee (with Vo Nguyen Giap as chairman), and the National Advisory Council (headed by Vinh Thuy).

After years of deterioration, the Opera House underwent extensive renovations from 1995 to 1997, restoring its former glory in preparation for the summit of the Francophonie Community. Today, the Hanoi Opera House remains one of the most important venues for performances in Hanoi, revered by artists as a sanctuary for classical art. Like many architectural structures and intangible cultural forms, the Hanoi Opera House serves as a testament to a period of cultural exchange in the city.

Historically, the site of the Hanoi Opera House was a swampy area belonging to the villages of Thach Tan and Tay Luong, bordering the village of Cuu Lau, part of the Huu Tuc district (or Phuc Lan district) of Tho Xuong county. In 1808, a mint (宝传局) was established in this region, with its northern boundary being today's Trang Tien Street, southern boundary being Pham Si Manh Street, eastern boundary being Phan Chu Trinh Street, and western boundary being Ngo Quyen Street. The northeast corner of the future opera house's swamp was the West Long Gate (or Tay Luong Gate), named after a ferry dock on the same-named riverbank during the Le dynasty. On July 20, 1786, the Western Mountain army entered Thang Long through this ferry and gate, attacking the Tran Khai lord's troops stationed at the Five Dragons Tower (now the location of the Hanoi Post Office). This ferry also witnessed the retreat of Qing forces led by Ton Si Nghi following their defeat at the Khuong Thuan fort by Admiral Long on January 30, 1789 (the fifth day of the lunar new year).
