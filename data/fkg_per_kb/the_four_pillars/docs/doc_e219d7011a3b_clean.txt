The highest-ranking leadership group in Vietnam's political system is referred to as 'The four pillars' or occasionally 'The five pillars' in colloquial terms. This term originates from the feudal period in Vietnam, where it was used to denote four key officials who were considered the pillars of the royal court, particularly during the dynasties of the Đinh and Nguyễn periods. Currently, the term 'The four pillars' is primarily used by public opinion, some media outlets, independent analysts, and non-official voices in Vietnam.

Throughout modern history, despite various changes, 'The four pillars' now refers to a group of top officials comprising the General Secretary兼任国家主席, the Prime Minister, the Chairman of the National Assembly, and the Standing Member of the Central Committee Secretariat. The positions of General Secretary and State President are the highest leadership roles within the Party and also serve as the head of state; the Prime Minister heads the government; the Chairman of the National Assembly leads the Vietnamese National Assembly and the Standing Committee of the National Assembly; and the Standing Member of the Central Committee Secretariat oversees daily operations of the Central Committee Secretariat.

During the Đinh Dynasty, the 'Four Pillars' consisted of four high-ranking officials: Nguyen Bac, an Imperial Chancellor; Dinh Dian, an External Minister; Trinh Tu, a Grand Secretary; and Liu Qi, a Grand Tutor. These four officials were revered by later generations as the 'Four Pillars' of the court. In the Tràng An World Heritage Site, there is a temple dedicated to these four great officials, known as the Temple of the Four Pillars. Additionally, at the Đinh Tiên Hoàng King Temple in the Hoa Lư Ancient Capital (Ninh Bình), there is a hall for incense offerings dedicated to the 'Four Pillars' of the court.

Under the Nguyễn Dynasty, the concept of 'The Four Pillars' referred to the four highest-ranking Grand Secretaries in the imperial court. These positions included the Needing Palace Grand Secretary, the Literary Brightness Palace Grand Secretary, the Martial Evident Palace Grand Secretary, and the Eastern Pavilion Grand Secretary. All four held the rank of First Rank Official, higher than Secretaries of State (Second Rank Officials, equivalent to today’s Ministers) and Governors. Typically, the emperor would appoint these four high-ranking officials as advisors and members of the Imperial Council. In times of crisis, they would automatically become Regency Advisors and form a Regency Council to manage state affairs when the emperor was absent or underage. Their assistants were called Associate Grand Secretaries.

After the August Revolution in 1945, Hồ Chí Minh declared the establishment of the Democratic Republic of Vietnam on September 2, 1945. Positions such as President and Premier (equivalent to Prime Minister) were established, with Hồ Chí Minh holding both titles. These positions were formalized by the Constitution of 1946, along with the role of Chairman of the Standing Committee of the National Assembly (equivalent to Chairman of the National Assembly). From that time until 1991 (excluding the period from 1960 to 1969), these four positions never simultaneously served on the Politburo, the most powerful body within the Communist Party of Vietnam. The positions of General Secretary and Premier have always been part of the Politburo since its inception. Meanwhile, the President from 1969 to 1981, and the Chairman of the National Assembly from 1946 to 1960 and 1981 to 1992, did not participate in the Politburo.
