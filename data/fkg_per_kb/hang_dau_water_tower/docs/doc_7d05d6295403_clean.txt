The Hang Dau Water Tower, originally named the Head Reservoir and also known as the Hang Dau Post Office or Round Water Plant, is located at Hang Dao Street in Ba Dinh District, Hanoi. The tower was constructed in 1894 along with the Tranquil Fort Water Tower, serving as a water supply facility for the city during the French colonial period and later becoming a historical relic of the capital.

In the context of its construction, on July 19, 1888, President of France Sadi Carnot issued an edict to establish Hanoi as a city, and from 1898, Hanoi became a French protectorate. Prior to the construction of the water tower, residents of Hanoi primarily relied on dug wells or water from numerous ponds scattered throughout the city. In the years 1894, several large outbreaks of diseases linked to water contamination occurred in the city, affecting many inhabitants, including twelve thousand French soldiers. The death of Paul Bert from dysentery shortly after his appointment as governor of Annam and Tonkin in 1896 prompted the French to urgently implement a European-style clean water system rather than relying on old water sources.

In 1896, the French established a special committee to survey local water sources as a priority in planning Hanoi. The decision to build the water plant (Yen Phu Water Plant) was mainly due to the unsanitary drinking water. The water project, along with the Hang Dau Water Tower and other urban water and electricity facilities, were primarily intended to serve the French colonists and soldiers, while the local population was restricted and only allowed access to excess water. Consequently, the Hang Dau Water Tower, part of the major water distribution system within the city, marked the first significant change in the face of Hanoi, predating prominent contemporary constructions such as the Long Bien Bridge and the Hanoi Opera House.

Construction of the Yen Phu Water Plant began in 1894, coinciding with the demolition of Hanoi's city walls by the French. Stones from the dismantled walls were purchased by businessman Tu Hong and sold for the construction of both the water plant and two water towers: one called the Head Reservoir built in 1894, situated at the northeast corner of the old city wall near where French troops were stationed, and another less famous tower called the Tail Reservoir (Tranquil Fort Water Tower) located in the Tranquil Fort concession area southeast of the city, close to the military hospital of that time (now part of the Central Military Hospital No. 108). The location of the Hang Dau Water Tower was deemed ideal for water distribution without requiring additional pumping, allowing water to flow directly into the Cua Bac Gate. The height of the Hang Dau Water Tower created sufficient pressure to deliver water to the French quarters and public faucets cast in iron placed at the ends of streets, supplying various government offices, villas, and private residences in the inner city.

Designed like a sturdy fortress, the Hang Dau Water Tower stands three stories high, featuring a cylindrical body and a conical roof, with a diameter of 19 meters and a height of 21 meters (25 meters including the roof). The interior space measures approximately 250 square meters, housing a steel reservoir on the top floor with a capacity of 1,250 cubic meters. Surrounding the tower are fifty-four narrow and tall windows resembling gun ports, complemented by arches and ornamental ironwork to avoid a heavy appearance. The entrance to the tower features two doors in the style of French architectural designs of the time, and atop the tower is a lightning rod.
