Portrait painting is a genre in painting where the primary goal is to represent a specific human subject. This form of art can also refer to the actual painted portrait itself. Historically, portraitists would create their works either by commission from wealthy patrons or out of admiration for their subjects. These portraits often served as significant historical records for both state and family archives, as well as personal mementos.

Traditionally, portrait paintings were mainly reserved for the rich and powerful. However, over time, middle-class individuals began commissioning portraits of their families and acquaintances. Today, portrait paintings continue to be commissioned by various entities including governments, corporations, groups, clubs, and individuals. Additionally, portraits can now be created using different media such as prints (etching and lithography), photography, video, and digital art.

The concept of achieving a recognizable likeness in a portrait has evolved gradually. It took many years for artists to develop the necessary skills to capture a good likeness of their subjects. According to Aristotle, the purpose of art is to convey the inner essence of the subject rather than merely their outward appearance. Artists often aim for a representative portrayal, as Edward Burne-Jones noted, emphasizing the importance of portraying character and moral qualities rather than transient expressions.

A well-executed portrait typically presents a serious, closed-lip stare, with subtle emotions conveyed through the eyes and eyebrows. The eyes are crucial for conveying the subject’s character, while the eyebrows can express a range of emotions. For instance, Gordon C. Aymar highlighted the role of the eyes in providing essential information about the subject, and the eyebrows in registering various emotional states.

Portraits can vary in size and composition, depicting the subject full-length, half-length, bust, or just the head. The subject's head can turn from a full face to a profile view, with a three-quarter view falling somewhere in between. Some portraits even feature unconventional poses, like Andrew Wyeth's "Christina's World," where the subject's back is turned to the viewer.

The choice of medium and color palette also varies widely. For example, Pierre-Auguste Renoir used a vibrant palette in his portrait "Mme. Charpentier and her children," while Gilbert Stuart employed a more restrained approach in his portrait of George Washington. The size of the portrait can also be significant, as seen in Chuck Close's large-scale museum pieces compared to smaller, more portable works.

Creating a portrait can be a lengthy process, often requiring several sessions with the subject. The final placement of the portrait, whether in a home or a museum, can influence its design and scale. Overall, portrait painting remains a versatile and enduring form of artistic expression, capturing the essence of individuals and groups across various contexts and media.
