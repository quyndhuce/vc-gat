The Vietnam National Museum of History, located in the Hoan Kiem district of Hanoi, stands as a testament to the rich historical heritage of Vietnam. Originally known as the Musée Louis-Finot, it served as a branch of the French School of the Far East during the colonial era. The museum building was inaugurated in 1910 and underwent extensive renovations in 1920 and again between 1925 and 1932, designed by architects Ernest Hébrard and Charles Batteur. This structure exemplifies a unique architectural style known as "Indochinois," blending elements of French colonial design with traditional Vietnamese aesthetics.

In 1958, the museum was acquired by the government of North Vietnam and officially opened its doors to the public on September 3, 1958. Since then, the collections have been expanded to encompass not only Eastern arts but also a comprehensive overview of Vietnamese national history. Today, the museum houses over 200,000 items, arranged in four major sections that trace the country’s historical timeline from prehistoric times to the modern era.

Situated at the rear of the Hanoi Opera House, the museum occupies a strategic location on 1 Trang Tien Street and 216 Tran Quang Khai Street. Its distinctive pagoda-like structure, complete with a cupola, features double walls and balconies designed to facilitate natural ventilation and protect against sunlight. The exhibition area spans more than 2,200 square meters, with exhibits displayed in chronological order.

The museum’s exhibits are divided into four primary sections. The first section covers the Stone Age, showcasing artifacts from approximately 40,000 years to 4,000 years ago. The second section focuses on the Tran dynasty, highlighting the period of nation-building. The third section delves into the history from the Ho dynasty through to the August 1945 Revolution, while the fourth section is dedicated to Champa stone sculptures.

Among the notable exhibits are Hung era and Neolithic mortuaries, Bronze Age tools such as axe heads, and artifacts from the Cham period. One particularly striking exhibit is the intimidating sculpture of Quan Am, the Goddess of Mercy, featuring 1,000 eyes and arms. Additionally, there are displays of the ornate thrones of the 13 Nguyễn dynasty emperors, along with dresses and other antiquities. Notable among these are the large Đông Sơn drums, symbols of Vietnamese culture, and various stone implements, pottery, and ornaments dating back to 1400 AD.

On the first floor, visitors can explore exhibits pertaining to the monarchic reigns from the Dinh and Lê eras around 900 AD to the last emperors of Vietnam. Decorative items such as mother-of-pearl inlaid chests, enamel cylindrical funerary jars, ceramics, and bright lacquer ware are featured prominently. The museum also houses a stele inscribed with an ancient dedication to God Harivamsesvara, discovered during an archaeological excavation.

To enhance public understanding of Vietnam’s historical narrative, the museum organized an exhibition in Austria and Belgium from September 2003 to November 2004, featuring 450 artifacts, including 416 from 13 Vietnamese museums and 34 from 8 European institutions. This exhibition, titled "Vietnam – Past and Present," was co-hosted by the Royal Museum of Arts and History in Belgium and the Vienna Ethnology Museum in Austria.
