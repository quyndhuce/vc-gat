Hang Đao Street, nestled within the bustling heart of Hanoi's ancient quarter, is a vibrant testament to the city’s rich history and enduring traditions. Originally part of the Thai Cu ward during the Le dynasty, and later belonging to Dong Lac, Dai Loc wards under the Tho Xuan district, Hang Đao Street has evolved into one of the most lively thoroughfares in the historic downtown area. The street’s name, "Hang Đào," translates to "Silk Street" and reflects its long-standing association with silk and textile crafts. Even during the colonial period, it was known as Rue de la Soie, further cementing its reputation as a hub for silk production and trade.

The origins of Hang Đao Street can be traced back to the early 15th century when Nguyen Trai, a prominent scholar and poet, mentioned it in his work "Dư Địa Chí." According to this historical account, Hang Đao was a place where artisans specialized in dyeing silk, particularly in shades of red, pink, and peach. Over time, the scope of their craft expanded from dyeing to bleaching silk, marking the beginning of a legacy that would endure through centuries.

Today, Hang Đao Street is not only a commercial center but also a repository of cultural heritage. Alongside the numerous shops selling silk products, there are several significant religious and historical sites. Among these are the Dong Lac Shrine at number 31, the Dong Lac Communal House at number 38, the Dai Loi Temple, also known as Bai Bo Temple at number 47, and the Hoa Loc Communal House at number 90A. These structures, each with its own unique architectural style and historical significance, contribute to the street’s rich tapestry of cultural life.

During the French colonial era, Hang Đao Street flourished as a marketplace for silk goods. The shops lining the street displayed a wide array of silk products, including voile, satin, mulberry silk, fine silk, gauze, brocade, and crepe. Every month, on the 1st and 6th days of the lunar calendar, a silk market called Chợ Hàng Tơ (Silk Market) was held, attracting traders and buyers from across the region. While some shops sold clothing, this was primarily done in nearby Hang Vải Street, leaving Hang Đao Street to focus on its core specialty of silk.

In the early 20th century, Indian merchants opened stores to sell imported silk and wool products, diversifying the range of goods available on the street. However, the primary offerings remained traditional silk products. This period also saw the establishment of general stores, jewelry shops, and hat-making establishments, though they did not overshadow the dominance of silk-related businesses.

For those interested in the revolutionary history of Vietnam, Hang Đao Street holds particular significance. It was here that the Dong Kinh Nghia Thuc School was founded by Ly Vu Can and his associates from March to December 1907. Notable figures like Phan Chu Trinh, a renowned anti-French revolutionary, resided briefly in the area and delivered many speeches advocating for change. The school’s influence spread rapidly, inspiring similar educational institutions in the outskirts of Hanoi. On October 10, 1954, after the liberation of Hanoi, Vietnamese troops marched triumphantly through Hang Đao Street, greeted by enthusiastic crowds waving flags and flowers.

In contemporary times, Hang Đao Street transforms into a vibrant night market every weekend, becoming a key attraction for tourists visiting the ancient quarter of Hanoi. Visitors can explore the myriad stalls offering fresh produce and traditional foods, immersing themselves in the daily rhythms of local life. Nearby, the Chau Long Market provides a glimpse into the simple yet vibrant lifestyle of Hanoi residents, while the historic Chợ Hôm - Đức Viên offers a vast selection of fabrics and traditional snacks. For those interested in silk, Hang Gai Street, known for its high-quality silk products, is just a short walk away, showcasing the enduring tradition of silk weaving in Vietnam.

Hang Đao Street’s atmosphere is a blend of old-world charm and modern vibrancy. The narrow lanes are lined with shops displaying colorful silk garments, intricate embroidery, and delicate fabrics. The sounds of haggling and the chatter of visitors mix seamlessly with the soft rustle of silk being handled by skilled artisans. The street’s architecture, with its ornate wooden doors and windows, adds to the visual appeal, creating a picturesque setting that is both inviting and enchanting.

Visitors to Hang Đao Street can experience the rich cultural heritage of Hanoi firsthand. They can wander through the bustling markets, admire the intricate designs of traditional silk products, and engage with the friendly locals who continue to uphold the age-old traditions of silk making. Whether it’s the rhythmic sound of looms or the vibrant colors of silk fabrics, Hang Đao Street offers a unique window into the past and present of Hanoi’s textile industry, making it a must-visit destination for anyone exploring the ancient quarter.

Situated to the north of Hoàn Kiếm Lake, Hang Đao Street stretches approximately 260 meters in length, running from north to south. It is one of the 36 streets that form the historic core of Old Quarter in Hanoi. To the south of Hang Đao Street lies the bustling Kinh Thiếc Market, while to the north it connects with Hang Ngang Street.

The name "Hang Đao" itself carries a rich historical significance. In ancient times, this street was known for selling silk and other colorful fabrics, making it a hub for textile merchants. From the 15th and 16th centuries, immigrants from various regions, particularly those from the Red River Delta, established themselves here, specializing in dyeing silk and weaving. This led to Hang Đao becoming a center for silk dyeing and weaving, renowned throughout Hanoi. Over time, the street attracted not only local residents but also visitors from neighboring villages such as La Cả, La Khê, and Lane Bai along the West Lake. Even today, one can find a stone tablet from 1706 that marks the establishment of the silk dyeing guild, which was the official guild of the street and symbolizes the prosperity of the craft community at No. 90A. Later, when the silk dyeing trade shifted to Hang Cuoi Street, Hang Đao transformed into a market for various goods including thread, yarn, lacquerware, bamboo, wood, paper, cotton, brocade, and more.

The southern end of Hang Đao Street is marked by the electric tram station, which once served as a vital transportation link connecting the city center to the flower market at Hang Dau. The tram line, built by the French colonialists, ran through the heart of the Old Quarter, providing a convenient mode of transport for both locals and tourists. Alongside the tram tracks, one can see numerous electric poles, remnants of the old tram system. The street is lined with two-story houses, showcasing the traditional architecture of Hanoi, characterized by their distinctive red-tiled roofs and ornate facades. These buildings reflect the vibrant history and culture of the area, offering a glimpse into the past.

In addition to its commercial activities, Hang Đao Street also holds significant cultural importance. It was the site of the founding of the Kinh Nghiem Street movement in 1907, initiated by Luong Van Can and other patriotic figures. At number 10 on the street, they established the movement aimed at awakening the Vietnamese people under French colonial rule. The objectives of the movement were to promote education and cultural exchange, advocating for free schooling and public performances to foster unity among the populace. However, the movement faced severe repression from the French authorities, leading to the arrest and imprisonment of Luong Van Can, who was later exiled to Co Lung. Another prominent figure, Luong Ngoc Quyen, was tragically killed during the Thai Nguyen uprising in 1917.

Visitors to Hang Đao Street can explore its rich history and cultural heritage through its architecture and the stories embedded within its walls. The street is adorned with traditional two-story houses, each with its unique charm and historical significance. For instance, the house at number 4 was once the residence of the family of Luong Van Can, while the house at number 10, just a short distance away, was the birthplace of the Kinh Nghiem Street movement. These structures stand as silent witnesses to the resilience and spirit of the Vietnamese people during the colonial era.

The atmosphere of Hang Đao Street is a blend of commerce and cultural heritage, where one can experience the hustle and bustle of daily life alongside the echoes of a storied past. The narrow lanes and vibrant markets offer a glimpse into the traditional crafts and trades that have sustained the community for generations. Visitors can wander through the streets, admiring the intricate details of the architecture, the colorful displays of goods in shop windows, and the lively interactions between vendors and customers. Each corner of the street tells a story, inviting travelers to immerse themselves in the rich tapestry of Hanoi's history and culture.
