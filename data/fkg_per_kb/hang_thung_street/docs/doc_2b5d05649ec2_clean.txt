Hang Thung Street, a historic thoroughfare in the heart of Hanoi, stretches from Tran Nhat Duat Street to Hang Be Street, encompassing what was once the land of the villages of So Trang and Dong Yen. By the mid-19th century, these two villages merged into one, known as Trang Luan. The temple and shrine of the village of Ta Lau now reside on Pho Lo Su, while those of So Trang are located at number 33 Hang Thung. Despite this, both are collectively referred to as the Trang Luan Temple and Shrine. The temple of Dong Yen faces the Trang Luan Temple across Nguyen Huu Huan Street, serving as both house number 35 Hang Thung and house number 94 Nguyen Huu Huan, dedicated to Uy Linh Lang. This temple was demolished in 1970.

Historically, Hang Thung Street was renowned for its production and sale of various types of barrels made from bamboo and rattan, or wooden barrels coated with lacquer. During the French colonial period, the street was divided into two sections: from Tran Nhat Duat to Nguyen Huu Huan Street was called Phuc Chau (Fou-tchéou) Street, later renamed Rondony Street. This area was home to numerous wood shops, where carpentry flourished, leading many merchants to amass wealth quickly. The wood trade expanded into the inner part of the street, beyond the original gate. Besides planks and boards, artisans crafted finished wooden products such as beds, wardrobes, tables, chairs, and food storage cabinets, even coffins. Wealthier residents renovated their old houses, constructing new ones that were taller, wider, and more aesthetically pleasing.

The remaining portion of the street, from Nguyen Huu Huan to Hang Be, was known as Hang Thung Street (Rue des Sceaux). After August 1945, both segments were combined under the name Phuc Chuan Street. In 1947, the Vietnamese government unified the two streets, reverting to the name Hang Thung, which it retains to this day.

At number 22 Hang Thung Street stands the Tho Nam Shrine, a spiritual hub for the local community. In 2008, artist Vu Van Thao's sculpture "Ancient House No. 13 Hang Thung Street" was among the top ten works in the APB Foundation Signature Art Prize for Asia-Pacific.

Like other streets in the ancient quarter of Hanoi, Hang Thung Street's name reflects its historical specialization in producing and selling barrels. These barrels were used for carrying water or storing fish sauce. On the eastern side of Hang Thung Street, now known as Rondony Street, many families operated wood shops and sold household goods made of wood. The carpentry industry thrived, contributing significantly to the prosperity of the residents. Old, low houses were transformed into newer, larger, and more beautiful structures.

Over time, the number of households engaged in barrel-making has dwindled, and today, no one continues this traditional craft. However, the enterprising residents have adapted by diversifying their businesses to align with contemporary trends. Today, Hang Thung Street is bustling with various commercial activities, including restaurants, grocery stores, and fashion boutiques. Notably, Madame Hong's nem tai shop at number 37 Hang Thung Street has become a beloved fixture for locals and visitors alike, preserving the unique flavors and traditions of the past.

Visitors to Hang Thung Street can immerse themselves in the rich tapestry of Hanoi’s history and culture. The street’s architecture, with its mix of old and new buildings, offers a glimpse into the evolution of urban life in Vietnam. The Tho Nam Shrine, with its serene atmosphere, provides a tranquil space for reflection and worship. The vibrant energy of the street, filled with the sounds of bustling commerce and the aromas of traditional cuisine, creates a memorable experience for tourists and locals alike.

Hang Thung Street, located in Hoan Kiem District of Hanoi, is a historic thoroughfare that stretches for 216 meters and is 8 meters wide. It runs from Tran Quang Khai Street to Hang Be Street, which connects with Cau Go Street, intersecting with Hang Tre Street and Nguyen Huu Huan Street. This street has a rich history dating back to the time when it was part of two separate villages, Sotrang and Dongyen, both belonging to the old Tatha district of Tho Xuong county. By the mid-19th century, these two villages merged into one, known as Tranglau, with their respective communal houses and temples still standing today.

During the French colonial period, Hang Thung Street was divided into two sections. The segment from Tran Nhut Duct Street to Nguyen Huu Huan Street was called Phuc Chau Street, later renamed Rondonny Street in 1919. The remaining portion retained its original name, Hang Thung Street, also known as Rue des Sceaux. After the revolution, Rondonny Street was renamed Binh Chuan Street, named after a former official responsible for foreign trade affairs. In 1945, the two streets were combined into one, retaining the name Hang Thung Street, which has remained unchanged since then.

Today, Hang Thung Street falls under the jurisdiction of Ly Thai To and Hang Bac wards in Hoan Kiem District. The street's cultural significance is evident in its historical landmarks, such as the communal house and temple of Tranglau village, located at number 33 on Hang Thung Street, though its main entrance faces Nguyen Huu Huan Street at number 77. Similarly, the communal house of Dongyen village, dedicated to the legendary hero Uy Linh Lang, stands at the intersection of Hang Thung and Nguyen Huu Huan Streets, specifically at number 35 Hang Thung Street and number 94 Nguyen Huu Huan Street. This structure was demolished around 1970, but its legacy remains deeply embedded in the local culture.

The origins of the street's name are rooted in the traditional crafts practiced here. Historically, this area was home to numerous workshops producing various types of wooden containers, such as water buckets and jars used for storing fish sauce. These products were made using bamboo and rattan, as well as wood joined with traditional lacquer, giving rise to the name "Hang Thung," meaning "Street of Buckets." The eastern end of the street was primarily occupied by timber merchants who sold planks and logs, while the junction with Hang Tre Street was a bustling market for bamboo and rattan products, known as the Hang Be Market.

The street's layout and architecture reflect its historical importance. It was once enclosed within the ancient walls of the Thang Long Citadel, with a gate called Dongyen Gate situated where Hang Thung Street meets Nguyen Huu Huan Street. This gate marked the eastern boundary of the citadel, and the area around it was a hub for artisans and traders involved in the production and sale of wooden containers and other goods. The preservation of these traditional crafts and the historical significance of the street make it a unique destination for visitors interested in the cultural heritage of Hanoi.

Visitors to Hang Thung Street can immerse themselves in the rich tapestry of Hanoi’s past, exploring the narrow lanes lined with old houses and shops that continue to carry on traditional crafts and trades. The atmosphere is one of quiet dignity, with the sounds of hammering and crafting echoing through the streets, reminiscent of the bustling activity of centuries past. The street's cultural significance extends beyond its physical presence, embodying the spirit of resilience and continuity that defines Hanoi's identity.
