Since April 2025, the Hanoi Museum has collaborated with the Institute of Archaeology and the Faculty of Social Sciences and Humanities at Vietnam National University, Hanoi, to refine and study the collection of artifacts and relics gathered from the excavation conducted in the western part of Vườn Chuối in 2024. These efforts have revealed a detailed picture of the settlement patterns and lifestyle of the ancient inhabitants of Vườn Chuối, highlighting the continuous expansion and development of the village through various historical periods.

The archaeological findings indicate that the initial settlement was established on elevated landforms, surrounded by natural waterways, which gradually expanded into lower areas. This transition reflects the sophisticated organization of the community, with evidence of specialized labor and advanced social structures. The site includes residential areas, burial grounds, and workshops, providing a comprehensive view of life in the region during the Bronze Age.

The Vườn Chuối site is a testament to the presence of early Vietnamese agricultural societies that settled and developed the Red River Delta nearly 4,000 years ago. It represents a crucial period in the formation of early Vietnamese states, showcasing the continuity of cultural practices and technological advancements across different epochs, including the Phung Nguyen, Dong Dau, Go Mun, Dong Son, and Post-Dong Son cultures.

The exhibition at the Hanoi Museum, titled "Discoveries from Vườn Chuối," comprises five sections: the journey of discovery, the convergence and crystallization of cultural traditions, the protection and promotion of the Vườn Chuối heritage, the contributions of scientists to the site, and an interactive experience area where visitors can simulate the work of an archaeologist. The site, which was first discovered in 1969, has been the subject of extensive archaeological excavations conducted by the Hà Nội Department of Culture and Sports in collaboration with specialized institutions. Over the years, these excavations have uncovered a wealth of artifacts and structures that provide invaluable insights into the lives of the people who lived in this area during various prehistoric periods, including the Phùng Nguyên, Đồng Đậu, Gò Mun, Đông Sơn, and Post-Eastern Sơn cultures.

One of the most significant findings from the recent excavation campaign, which began in March 2024 and is set to conclude in March 2025, is the discovery of a village that developed continuously over nearly 4,000 years. This excavation, covering an area of 6,000 square meters, has revealed important traces of daily life, residential areas, workshops for crafting stone and wooden tools, burial sites, and numerous artifacts characteristic of the Pre-Eastern Sơn and Eastern Sơn periods. Among the remarkable discoveries are jade greenstone axes, symbolic of the power of tribal leaders, and other precious relics that have never before been recorded in the archaeological research of the Metal Age in northern Vietnam and Hà Nội.

The site's importance is further underscored by the variety of artifacts found, including jade ornaments such as necklaces, earrings, beads, and pendants that were likely worn by the elite members of society. These items not only reflect the artistic skills of the ancient inhabitants but also provide clues about their social structure and beliefs. Additionally, the excavation has unearthed a range of tools and pottery, which offer a window into the daily activities and technological advancements of the time.
