Ngoc Son Temple, a revered spiritual site nestled within the heart of Hanoi's historic Hoan Kiem Lake, stands as one of the city’s most cherished landmarks. The temple is situated on a small island known as the Jade Islet, which is part of the picturesque Hoan Kiem Lake, located in the northeastern corner of the lake. This serene setting, surrounded by water, creates a harmonious blend of natural beauty and cultural significance, making Ngoc Son Temple a must-visit destination for both locals and tourists alike.

The origins of Ngoc Son Temple date back to the 19th century when it was initially established as a pagoda named Chua Ngoc Son. Over time, it underwent several transformations and renovations, eventually becoming a temple dedicated to the worship of two significant figures in Vietnamese history: General Tran Hung Dao, a national hero who defeated the Mongol invaders in the 13th century, and Van Xuan De Quan, the deity associated with scholarly pursuits and imperial examinations. The temple's name itself reflects its storied past, having been renamed from Ngoc Tuong to Ngoc Son during the Tran Dynasty, symbolizing the reverence for heroes and martyrs who fought against foreign invaders.

In 1865, under the guidance of scholar Nguyen Van Sieu, Ngoc Son Temple underwent a major restoration that significantly altered its structure and added new elements. This renovation included the construction of the Tran Ba Pavilion, the Thap But (Pen Tower), and the Dai Nien (Inkstone Pavilion). These additions not only enhanced the physical appearance of the temple but also enriched its cultural and historical value. The Pen Tower, in particular, stands as a testament to the importance of education and literature in Vietnamese culture, while the Inkstone Pavilion symbolizes the scholarly pursuits that were central to the lives of scholars and officials during the imperial era.

The architectural design of Ngoc Son Temple is characterized by its unique layout, which follows the shape of the Chinese character "Tam," representing the concept of harmony and balance. As visitors approach the temple, they are greeted by a beautifully adorned wall featuring dragon and tiger motifs, along with poetic couplets that reflect the values of learning and examination. Inside the temple, the intricate details of its architecture are evident in every nook and cranny, showcasing the traditional craftsmanship and artistic skills of Vietnamese artisans. The temple's structure is further complemented by the presence of various shrines dedicated to different deities, reflecting the syncretic religious beliefs of Vietnam, where Buddhism, Confucianism, and Taoism coexist harmoniously.

The origins of Hoan Kiem Lake date back to the 15th century when it was known as Lake Luc Thuong due to its year-round greenish hue. The lake’s current name, Hoan Kiem, translates to "Lake Returning the Sword," derived from a legendary tale involving King Le Loi and a divine turtle. According to folklore, King Le Loi received a magical sword from the Dragon King to defeat the Ming invaders. After his victory, he returned the sword to the Dragon King by throwing it into the lake, where a giant turtle emerged to retrieve it. This event gave rise to the lake’s alternative names, Hoan Kiem and Lake Gau, reflecting the mythical connection between the king and the sacred weapon.

The temple itself is a blend of historical reverence and architectural beauty. Its construction began around 1864 under the guidance of Nguyen Van Sieu, a patriotic scholar who undertook extensive renovations to restore and enhance the site. The temple complex now comprises several key structures, each contributing to its overall aesthetic and spiritual ambiance. The entrance to Ngoc Son Temple is marked by the Thap But (Pen Tower), a slender tower that symbolizes the pen used by scholars for writing examinations. Beyond this tower lies the Nghi Mon (Gate of Ceremonial Entry), which leads visitors through a series of courtyards and pavilions, including the Dai Nien (Inkstone Pavilion) and the Thay Huc Bridge, named for its poetic association with the morning sun.
