Hang Trong Street, located in Hoan Kiem District of Hanoi, is a vibrant thoroughfare that stretches for 431 meters and spans a width of 8 meters. The street begins at the intersection of Hang Gai and Hang Bong streets and ends at Le Thai To Street, situated west of Hoan Kiem Lake. This historic street has been a witness to centuries of cultural and commercial activity, embodying the rich heritage of Hanoi.

The origins of Hang Trong Street can be traced back to several old villages, including the land adjacent to Hang Gai Street which was part of the ancient village of Co Vu, while the middle section belonged to the village of Khanh Thuy Huu, and the final segment was part of the village of Tu Thap. All these areas were once part of the ancient district of Tien Tuc, later renamed Thuong My, within the former district of Tho Xuan. During the Ly and Tran dynasties, this area was known as Phuong Tang Kiem, reflecting its significant role in the urban landscape of Hanoi.

When the French colonizers first occupied Hanoi, they referred to Hang Trong Street as Rue des Brodeurs, or Street of Embroiderers. It was one of the earliest streets in Hanoi to undergo reconstruction in a European style during the colonial period. Despite extensive renovations and beautification efforts, the street only achieved a relatively clean appearance by 1884. In 1889, the last thatched houses in Hanoi were demolished from this street and from what is now Trang Tien Street. By 1890, the street had been renamed Rue Jules Ferry, and it retained this name until 1945 when it was officially named Hang Trong Street. Since then, the name has remained unchanged through subsequent years in 1949 and 1951.

Today, Hang Trong Street is home to two significant religious sites: the Dong Huong Temple, also known as the Hang Trong Temple, and the Nam Huong Shrine. The Dong Huong Temple, located at number 82 on the street, is dedicated to a courtesan who was revered for her exceptional singing voice. There are two versions of her tragic story. According to the first account, she was a talented and beautiful woman whose singing voice led to her being captured by the Trinh lords and forced into servitude. Her beauty and talent eventually led to her execution by the jealous wife of the lord, who ordered her to be buried alive. However, her spirit avenged her death by punishing the lord's wife. The second version tells of a courtesan who cleverly outwitted invading Ming soldiers by luring them into sacks meant to protect them from mosquitoes, subsequently drowning them in a river. For this heroic deed, she was honored with a temple.

At the end of Hang Trong Street, at number 75, stands the Nam Huong Shrine, which honors the gods Bai Ma and Linh Lang. This shrine is closely associated with the nearby Hang Buom and Cau Giay areas, adding to the rich tapestry of spiritual and cultural landmarks in the region.

Historically, Hang Trong Street extended further to the intersection of Trang Thi Street, making it longer than the current segment ending at Le Thai To Street. The police station, now the headquarters of the Hoan Kiem Police Department, was originally called the Poste Commissariat Hang Trong. Nearby, the site of the Intimex Company building (numbers 30-32 Le Thai To Street) was once the largest hotel in Hanoi, Hotel du Lac, which operated from 1889 to 1945. This location also served as the headquarters for the Chamber of Commerce and Agriculture and the Higher School of Commerce during the colonial era.

During the anti-American resistance period (1955-1975), a house near the Nam Huong Shrine was converted into a club for southern cadres who had relocated to the north. Originally, this building was the headquarters of the Khai Tri Tien Duc Society, established by the French and local officials in 1919 to legitimize colonial rule. After the revolution succeeded, the building became the headquarters of the Cultural Rescue Association and later served as the office of the Standing Committee of the National Assembly from January 6, 1946, until the early 1970s. Today, it houses the Bureau of Grassroots Culture under the Ministry of Culture, Sports, and Tourism.

Hang Trong Street is renowned for its traditional crafts, particularly the production of drums and paper fans. The drum-making tradition was brought to the street by residents from Lien Thang Village, now part of Yen My District in Hai Hung Province. Here, various types of drums are crafted and sold, including large and small drums, ceremonial drums, and portable drums. Similarly, the art of fan-making was introduced by artisans from Dao Xa Village in Thanh Xuan District, who specialized in creating fans, parasols, and umbrellas for officials and temples. Another significant craft is the printing and hand-painting of Hang Trong paintings, a unique form of folk art originating from the village of Tu Thap. These paintings are characterized by their intricate designs and vibrant colors, often depicting themes such as mythical creatures, historical figures, and seasonal motifs. The paintings are created using woodblocks for the outlines and hand-painted for the colors, resulting in a wide range of hues and artistic styles.

The street retains much of its colonial-era architecture, with buildings featuring ornate facades and balconies. The presence of the Dong Huong Temple and Nam Huong Shrine adds a spiritual dimension to the area, offering visitors a glimpse into the religious practices and beliefs of the local community. The street's proximity to Hoan Kiem Lake enhances its appeal, providing a serene backdrop against which the bustling activities of the street unfold.

Hang Trong Street, a historic district nestled within the bustling heart of Hoan Kiem District in Hanoi, is a vibrant tapestry woven with threads of history, culture, and commerce. This charming area, known for its narrow streets lined with ancient trees and quaint alleyways, is a testament to the city's rich heritage and enduring traditions. The street's name, Hang Trong, evokes images of a bygone era when merchants would transport silk and other goods through these thoroughfares, often accompanied by the rhythmic sounds of bamboo poles used to carry their wares. Over time, this name became synonymous with the bustling trade and cultural activities that define the area today.

The origins of Hang Trong Street can be traced back to the early 12th century, during the reign of the Ly Dynasty, when it was established as a significant commercial hub within the ancient capital of Thang Long. As the centuries passed, the area evolved into a thriving center of economic activity, housing numerous shops, boutiques, and workshops that catered to the needs of both locals and visitors alike. Today, Hang Trong Street stands as a living museum, where one can witness the seamless blend of old-world charm and modern vibrancy.

Among the notable landmarks in Hang Trong Street is the majestic St. Joseph's Cathedral, a stunning example of neo-Gothic architecture built by the French in the late 19th century. Its towering spires and intricate stained glass windows stand out against the backdrop of the bustling streets below, offering a glimpse into the colonial past of Hanoi. Nearby, the Temple of Ly Trieu Quoc Su, with its golden tiles and ornate carvings, provides a serene sanctuary amidst the urban hustle and bustle. This temple, dating back to the 12th century, is a revered site that continues to attract devotees and tourists alike, who come to pay homage and admire its exquisite craftsmanship.

The cultural significance of Hang Trong Street extends beyond its religious sites to include a rich array of traditional crafts and local cuisine. One of the most iconic symbols of the area is the famous Hang Trong paintings, which have been a source of pride for generations. These intricate artworks, often depicting scenes from daily life and folklore, are a testament to the artistic talent and creativity of the local artisans. While the original hand-painted works are now rare, the tradition lives on through reproductions and modern interpretations that continue to captivate visitors.

In addition to its artistic treasures, Hang Trong Street is also home to several historic buildings and structures that reflect the architectural styles of different eras. The People's Committee Building, with its grandiose facade and lush gardens, stands as a symbol of civic governance and community spirit.
