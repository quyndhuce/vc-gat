Hang Tre Street, a historic thoroughfare in the heart of Hanoi, is steeped in centuries of tradition and transformation. Stretching approximately 300 meters from Hang Mam Street to Lo Su Street, this narrow lane has been a witness to the evolution of Vietnamese urban life, embodying the essence of the ancient city's vibrant past. Originally part of the Tran Thanh ward, which was later renamed Phuc Lam, Hang Tre Street stands as a testament to the rich tapestry of Hanoi’s history.

The street's name, Hang Tre, translates to Bamboo Street, derived from its early days when it was lined with bamboo groves and served as a hub for bamboo-related activities. The area was once a bustling center for the sale and processing of bamboo, with residents using the versatile material to construct houses, furniture, and even boats. This natural resource was integral to the daily lives of the people who lived and worked here, reflecting the deep-rooted connection between the community and their environment.

In the late 19th century, the street underwent significant changes. Initially known as Hang Cau due to the concentration of merchants selling cau fruit and cau wood transported by boat from various provinces, the French colonial era saw the relocation of these traders to nearby Hang Be Street. During this period, the French referred to the street as rue des Bambous, a translation of its Vietnamese name. After the August Revolution in 1945, the street reclaimed its original Vietnamese name, Hang Tre.

One of the most notable landmarks on Hang Tre Street is the former site of the first courthouse in Hanoi, established in 1884. Following the French occupation of Hanoi, the court became the central administrative body for legal matters in the northern and central regions of the city. In 1896, after the construction of a new courthouse at Carreau Street (now Ly Thuong Kiet Street), the old courthouse on Hang Tre Street was repurposed as the headquarters for the Ministry of Public Works. Despite this change, the area continued to be known as the Hang Tre Courthouse, a name that resonates with locals to this day.

Today, the Hang Tre Courthouse stands at the intersection of Hang Tre and Lo Su Streets, having undergone numerous renovations and expansions over the years. It now serves as the headquarters for two important agencies: the Ministry of Water Resources and the Central Criminal Investigation Department. The building itself is a blend of colonial architecture and modern additions, reflecting the layers of history embedded within its walls.

As Hang Tre Street entered the 20th century, the bamboo trade gradually declined, giving way to other commercial activities. The once-bustling marketplaces and workshops were transformed into rental spaces, catering to a variety of businesses. By the 1950s, the street had become a hub for motorcycle repair shops, with mechanics offering services such as oil changes, tire repairs, and general maintenance. This shift marked a significant transition in the economic landscape of the area, aligning with the changing needs of the growing city.

During the 1970s and 1980s, Hang Tre Street experienced further development, with many of the old buildings being renovated and modernized. The street became known for its diverse range of services, including bicycle and motorcycle repair shops, beauty salons, and small convenience stores. Today, visitors can find a plethora of shops offering everything from motorcycle repairs to beauty treatments, creating a lively atmosphere that reflects the dynamic nature of contemporary Hanoi.

Despite these changes, Hang Tre Street retains much of its historical charm. The narrow lanes and traditional architecture provide a glimpse into the past, while the bustling activity of modern commerce adds a layer of vibrancy. The street is also home to several high-quality noodle shops, serving up delicious and affordable dishes that cater to both locals and tourists alike. These culinary offerings not only reflect the local cuisine but also contribute to the street's reputation as a place where one can experience the authentic flavors of Hanoi.

Visitors to Hang Tre Street are treated to a unique blend of old and new, where the echoes of the past resonate through the present-day hustle and bustle. The street's rich history, coupled with its current role as a vibrant commercial center, makes it a must-visit destination for those interested in exploring the layered heritage of Hanoi. From the colonial-era courthouse to the modern-day shops, every corner of Hang Tre Street tells a story of resilience, adaptation, and enduring cultural significance.

Hang Tre Street, located in Hoan Kiem District of Hanoi, is a historic thoroughfare that stretches for 306 meters and is 8 meters wide. It runs from Hang Mam Street to Lo Su Street, intersecting with Bai Thai Buoi Alley and Hang Tong Street. The street's name has roots dating back to before the French colonial era, when it was known as "rue des Bambous" by the French. In 1945, the Vietnamese reclaimed its original name, Hang Tre, which it has retained ever since.

The origins of Hang Tre Street can be traced back to the time when this area was part of Trung Thanh, one of ten villages within the ancient Tả Túc (later renamed Phuc Lam) district of Tho Xuong County. The name "Hang Tre," meaning Bamboo Street, reflects the historical significance of bamboo and reed trade in this location. Due to its proximity to the Red River, the street was ideal for the transportation and handling of bamboo and reeds, leading to the establishment of stalls selling these materials, which gave rise to the street's name.

During the early years of French colonial rule, a significant change occurred on Hang Tre Street. The colonial authorities constructed a mansion in the middle of the street, which they used as a courthouse. However, in 1906, the court was relocated to what is now Ly Thuong Kiet Street, and the former courthouse became the headquarters of the Indochina Public Administration Office. Despite this, locals continued to refer to the building as the Hang Tre Court. Today, the site houses the Mekong River Commission office.

In addition to its historical significance, Hang Tre Street also witnessed the transformation of its southern end into a different commercial hub. For a period, this section was known as Hang Cau Street due to the presence of shops selling dried betel nuts. Over time, these businesses moved to the beginning of Hang Be Street, leaving Hang Tre Street with its original identity intact.

The street's cultural and historical importance extends beyond its commercial activities. It is home to a number of traditional crafts and trades that have been passed down through generations. The atmosphere of Hang Tre Street is characterized by the bustling activity of traders and shoppers, creating a vibrant scene that reflects the rich cultural heritage of Hanoi. Visitors can experience the unique ambiance of the street, where the sounds of vendors calling out their goods blend seamlessly with the hum of daily life.
