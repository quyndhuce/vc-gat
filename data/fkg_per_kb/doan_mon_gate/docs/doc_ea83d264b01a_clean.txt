Doan Mon Gate stands as one of the primary entrances to the Forbidden City, a testament to the grandeur and historical significance of ancient Vietnamese architecture. The gate's current structure was built during the Le Dynasty and underwent renovations during the Nguyen Dynasty, reflecting a blend of historical periods and architectural styles. Situated at the southern end of the Kinh Thien Palace, it aligns perfectly with the Hanoi Flag Tower, creating a harmonious axis that symbolizes the imperial power and authority of the royal court.

The gate is constructed horizontally in the shape of a U, with its main structure resembling a watchtower, featuring three arched gates. This design not only adds aesthetic appeal but also demonstrates advanced engineering techniques, as the arches provide excellent structural support. The central gate, reserved exclusively for the emperor, is the largest, measuring 4 meters in height and 2.7 meters in width. On either side, four smaller gates allow access for officials and members of the royal family when summoned for important ceremonies or rituals held at the Kinh Thien Palace by the Emperor.

Constructed primarily from brick and stone, the materials used reflect the prevalent building practices of the Le Dynasty. The bricks, known as "gach vo," were widely used during this period, while the arches are made of stone, showcasing the intricate craftsmanship of the time. The overall dimensions of the gate span 47.5 meters from east to west and 13 meters from north to south, with each wing extending an additional 26.5 meters. The height of the structure reaches 6 meters, creating a formidable presence that commands respect and awe.

Above the central gate, a plaque carved from stone bears the inscription "Doan Mon," measuring 1.5 meters in length and 0.7 meters in width. Steps made of small bricks lead up to the second level, where the area corresponds to the size of the main gate. Although this upper level has been repurposed as a military office, the original architectural details remain obscured. However, the rooftop features a distinctive two-tiered octagonal pavilion, adorned with dragon heads at the corners and decorative motifs such as hau phu dragons on the eaves. The roof tiles are traditional Vietnamese tiles, adding to the gate's regal appearance.

In 1999, archaeologists conducted excavations directly within the central gate of Doan Mon, uncovering significant historical artifacts. At a depth of 1.2 meters, they discovered a stone border marking the foundation of the gate, along with a courtyard paved with bricks typical of the Le Dynasty. Deeper still, at 1.9 meters, remnants of a path lined with "hoa chanh" bricks, characteristic of the Tran Dynasty, were found. This continuity underscores the gate's role as a crucial entry point to the Forbidden City, serving as a gateway to the heart of the imperial complex.

The gate's imposing structure and detailed craftsmanship offer a glimpse into the grandeur of past dynasties, while the surrounding area provides a tranquil setting for reflection and appreciation of Vietnam's cultural heritage.
