Doan Mon Gate is the main southern entrance to the Imperial Citadel of Thang Long, originally constructed during the Ly dynasty but the existing gate was built by the Early Le dynasty in the 15th century and subsequently repaired and renovated during the Nguyen dynasty in the 19th century. It is one of the five remaining structures from Thang Long that still exist today, alongside the Hanoi Flag Tower, the North Gate (Hanoi City Wall), the foundation of the Kinh Thien Palace, and the Temple of Literature - National University. The gate has a U-shaped structure stretching from east to west for 46.5 meters, featuring five archways. The central archway is reserved for the emperor's passage, while the two side archways on either end are known as Left Doan Mon and Right Doan Mon. The current gate remains intact and serves as the primary entry and exit point for the Imperial Citadel of Thang Long.

Additionally, there is another structure named Doan Mon located in Beijing, China. This Doan Mon is a gate within the Forbidden City complex, situated south of the Forbidden City. Heading north from the entrance of the Imperial City, it is the second gate after Tiananmen Gate (Tian An Men), and its architectural style resembles that of Tiananmen Gate. The next gate further north is the Ngo Mon Gate, which is the main southern gate of the Forbidden City. Constructed in the 18th year of Yongle during the Ming Dynasty (1420), the structure and architectural style of Doan Mon are similar to those of Tiananmen Gate. In the sixth year of Kangxi's reign (1666), Doan Mon was rebuilt. According to "The History of the Imperial Palace, Volume 11, Palace 1, Outer Court," it states: "The northern gate of Tiananmen is called Doan Mon, which was rebuilt in the sixth year of Kangxi's reign and has a system similar to Tiananmen. There are two clock towers (flower pavilions)." To the south, there are twenty porticos on both sides. To the east is the Tai Mieu Gate, and to the west is the Altar of Land and Grain Gate. The Doan Mon tower primarily stored ceremonial objects used by emperors during the Ming and Qing dynasties. During major ceremonies and important festivals, various types of books were displayed on both sides of the imperial path under the Doan Mon Gate, extending from the Dinh Thai Hoa to Tiananmen (known as Chengtianmen during the Ming Dynasty), covering a distance of two miles. Drums and bells were struck on the Doan Mon and Ngo Mon towers.
