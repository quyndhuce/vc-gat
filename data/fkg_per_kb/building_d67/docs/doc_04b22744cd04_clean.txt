Building D67, situated within the grounds of the Thang Long Imperial Citadel in Ba Dinh District, Hanoi, stands as a testament to the resilience and strategic importance of Vietnam’s military leadership during the tumultuous years of the Vietnam War. Constructed in 1967 under the auspices of the Democratic Republic of Vietnam, this structure embodies the country’s determination to maintain operational continuity despite relentless US aerial bombardments. The building, with its simple yet robust design, reflects the wartime military architectural style, characterized by thick concrete walls and a flat roof, ensuring both strength and privacy. The structure’s low, block-like form and muted color palette blend seamlessly into the historic ambiance of the citadel, emphasizing functionality over aesthetic appeal.

Located within the Thang Long Imperial Citadel, Building D67 is part of Area A, a critical zone that served as the nerve center for the Politburo and the Central Military Commission during pivotal moments in Vietnam’s revolutionary history. It was here that significant decisions were made, including those related to the Tet Offensive of 1968, the General Offensive of 1972, and the decisive campaigns of 1975 that led to the fall of Saigon. The building’s strategic importance is further underscored by its underground bunker system, known as D67 bunker, which lies beneath the courtyard connecting Kinh Thien Palace and the building itself. This bunker, accessible via two passages originating from the offices of Generals Vo Nguyen Giap and Vo Van Dien, is a labyrinth of reinforced concrete steps and chambers designed to withstand even the most intense bombings.

The D67 bunker, a marvel of military engineering, comprises a series of interconnected rooms, each meticulously constructed to serve specific functions. The meeting room, a solid rectangular block with a tiled floor and a single entrance, is flanked by adjacent rooms designated for the secretariat and machinery, including radio equipment. The ventilation and air filtration system, a massive installation manufactured in the Soviet Union, ensures a breathable environment within the bunker. The entrances and exits to the bunker are secured by six thick, blue-painted steel doors, each equipped with multiple handles and rubber gaskets to prevent water and toxic gases from entering.

The entire underground bunker system is powered by generator lines, providing uninterrupted electricity for communication, liaison, logistics, and food supplies. This intricate network of tunnels and rooms, designed to protect the highest echelons of military and political leadership, exemplifies the foresight and ingenuity of Vietnam’s defense planners. The construction of the D67 bunker and its associated facilities was a collaborative effort involving approximately 300 specialized cadres from various regiments, who worked tirelessly under strict security protocols to complete the project within six months.

During periods of heightened threat, the Politburo and the Central Military Commission would descend into the D67 bunker, where they could continue their operations undeterred by external threats. The bunker’s role in safeguarding Vietnam’s leadership during the war underscores its critical importance in the nation’s struggle for independence and reunification. Today, visitors to the Thang Long Imperial Citadel can explore the D67 House and its underground bunker, gaining insight into the historical context and the architectural ingenuity that contributed to Vietnam’s victory.

Recognized as part of the UNESCO World Heritage-listed Thang Long Imperial Citadel Complex and a national special relic, Building D67 serves not only as a symbol of Vietnam’s military prowess but also as a poignant reminder of the sacrifices made during the war. Open daily from 8:00 to 17:00, the site charges an admission fee of approximately 30,000 VND, offering tourists and history enthusiasts a unique opportunity to delve into the rich tapestry of Vietnam’s past.
