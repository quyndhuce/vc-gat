Hang Chieu Street, located in Hoan Kiem District of Hanoi, is a historic thoroughfare that stretches for 276 meters and spans a width of 8 meters. The street's origins trace back to the land of Thanh Ha village, which was part of the Hau Tuc district (later renamed Dong Xuan) within the old Tho Xuan county. This area has been a significant part of Hanoi’s urban landscape since ancient times, with its history deeply intertwined with the city’s development and cultural heritage.

The street's name, Hang Chieu, reflects its historical significance as a trading hub for bamboo mats and other similar goods. It is believed that the street's original name was Dong Ha, derived from the nearby Dong Ha ward, which was one of the 36 wards of Thang Long during the time when the city was divided into these administrative units. The name Hang Chieu itself likely evolved from the trade of bamboo mats, known as "chieu" in Vietnamese, which were a staple product in the region. Additionally, the street was also known as Hang Bat, indicating that it once traded not only bamboo mats but also ceramic wares, particularly those from Bat Trang, a renowned pottery village near Hanoi.

The street's historical importance is further highlighted by its proximity to the Quan Chauong gate, an ancient city gate that may have existed as early as the Le dynasty. According to historical records, the gate was part of the defensive walls built around the capital city of Thang Long in 1749 during the reign of King Le Duc Tong. The gate's name, Quan Chauong, is shrouded in legend, with various theories suggesting its origin. One theory posits that the name comes from a retired official who settled near the gate, while another suggests it was named after a guard who oversaw the gate during the Nguyen dynasty. Yet another theory claims the name honors a soldier who died defending the gate against French invaders in 1873. Despite these legends, the most accurate historical reference to the gate is found in Chinese characters inscribed above its arch, which read "Dong Ha Men," meaning "Eastern Ha Gate."

The street's architecture and layout reflect its historical evolution. Originally, the Thanh Ha communal house stood near the Quan Chauong gate until 1817 when the gate was expanded, necessitating the relocation of the communal house to its current site at number 77 Hang Chieu Street. The communal house faces the Ngoc Gach alley, where it stands as a testament to the area's rich cultural heritage. Inside, the communal house venerates Tran Luu, a legendary figure credited with heroic deeds during the resistance against Mongol invaders.

Hang Chieu Street's cultural significance extends beyond its historical landmarks. It was once the site of important rituals, such as the annual "spring ox" ceremony, which was held in the Dong Ha ward, now part of Hang Chieu. This ceremony, documented in historical texts like the Dai Nam Nhat Thong Chi, involved the presentation of a clay ox to the local community, symbolizing agricultural prosperity and fertility. The ox was then paraded through the streets before being ceremonially presented to the royal court, reflecting the deep-rooted traditions and agricultural practices of the region.

In modern times, Hang Chieu Street has continued to play a vital role in Hanoi's cultural and political life. During the early days of the national resistance war against the French, the street served as the headquarters for the First Regional Command, a strategic location that underscored its importance as a center of resistance. In January 1947, amidst intense fighting, a New Year's Eve dinner was hosted at number 87b Hang Chieu Street, organized by the command to gather foreign guests and explain the just cause of the Vietnamese resistance. This event showcased the resilience and unity of the local population, countering French propaganda that suggested the resistance movement was weakening.

Today, Hang Chieu Street remains a vibrant part of Hanoi's urban fabric, offering visitors a glimpse into the city's rich history and cultural traditions. Its narrow lanes lined with traditional shops and homes evoke a sense of continuity with the past, while the sounds and sights of daily life provide a vivid backdrop to the street's enduring charm. Tourists can explore the area's historical sites, including the Thanh Ha communal house, and experience the unique atmosphere of this historic thoroughfare, where the echoes of centuries-old traditions blend seamlessly with the rhythms of contemporary life.

Hang Chieu Street, a vibrant artery of Hanoi's ancient quarter, stretches for 280 meters from the Quan Chau Gate to the intersection of Dong Xuan, Hang Ma, and Hang Duong streets. This historic thoroughfare, nestled within the heart of the city, carries a rich tapestry of history, culture, and commerce that has evolved over centuries. The street's origins trace back to the time when it was part of Thanh Ha village, located in the old Hau Tuc district, which later became Dong Xuan district. The name "Hang Chieu" is derived from the tradition of selling smooth mats or "chiếu," which were imported by waterways from nearby villages and sold at the nearby To River port.

In the late 19th century, during the colonial era, Hang Chieu Street played a significant role in the French occupation of Hanoi. In 1873, Francis Garnier led an expedition into Hang Chieu, where he encountered fierce resistance at the Dong Ha gate, now known as the Quan Chau gate. A valiant officer named Chau Co, along with approximately 100 soldiers, fought bravely but ultimately perished in this battle. This event marked a turning point in the history of the street, leading to its renaming as the Quan Chau gate in honor of the fallen hero.

The French colonial period brought substantial changes to Hang Chieu Street. In 1888, after the French had secured control of Hanoi, they razed the entire street and rebuilt it according to their urban planning ideals. The new design included wide sidewalks, trees, and streetlights, earning it the nickname "New Street." During the early 1900s, the French renamed the street Jean Dupuis, after a famous French explorer, while the section near the Quan Chau gate was called Nattes en Joncs, or Reed Mats Street. By 1945, these two sections were merged back into a single street known as Hang Chieu, and in 1949, it was officially divided into Hang Chieu Street and Quan Chau Street as we know them today.

Throughout its history, Hang Chieu Street has been home to various businesses and activities. In the colonial era, the street was known for its arms dealers who supplied weapons and ammunition to the French army, making the area heavily guarded by the colonial authorities. After the August Revolution of 1945, Hang Chieu Street became a strategic location during the national resistance war. It served as the command center for the First Military Region until February 17, 1947, when the Capital Division withdrew to the outskirts of the city.

Today, Hang Chieu Street is bustling with activity, serving as one of the most vibrant commercial areas in the Old Quarter. The primary goods sold here are various types of mats, reflecting the enduring tradition of mat-making that has been passed down through generations. While traditional reed mats remain popular, modern varieties such as plastic mats, bamboo mats, and wooden grain mats have also found their place among the offerings. Alongside mats, visitors can find a range of handmade crafts made from reeds and bamboo, including baskets, hats, and decorative items.

In addition to mats, Hang Chieu Street is also known for its array of other products like banners, nylon bags, ropes, quilts, and floor coverings. The street's culinary scene adds another layer of charm, particularly with its reputation as a hub for "văn thắn" noodles, a type of wonton noodle soup that is a beloved local delicacy. Numerous noodle shops line the street, offering visitors a taste of authentic Hanoi cuisine.

Visiting Hang Chieu Street provides a unique glimpse into the rich cultural heritage of Hanoi. The atmosphere is lively and filled with the sounds of vendors calling out their wares, the sight of colorful mats displayed in shop windows, and the aroma of freshly cooked noodles wafting from the many eateries. For tourists, exploring Hang Chieu Street means immersing oneself in the traditional crafts and daily life of the Old Quarter, experiencing firsthand the blend of historical significance and contemporary vibrancy that defines this storied street.
