A Performance is an act or process of staging or presenting a play, concert, or other forms of entertainment. It can also be understood as the action or process of carrying out or accomplishing an action, task, or function. In the workplace, job performance is the hypothesized conception or requirements of a role, divided into two types: contextual and task. Task performance is dependent on cognitive ability, while contextual performance is influenced by personality. Task performance relates to behavioral roles recognized in job descriptions and remuneration systems, directly impacting organizational performance. Contextual performance, on the other hand, involves value-based contributions that are not explicitly covered by job descriptions or compensation, adding indirect support to organizational performance. Citizenship performance, similar to contextual performance, refers to individual activities that support organizational culture.

In the realm of performing arts, a Performance generally involves an event where a performer or group of performers present one or more works of art to an audience. In instrumental music and drama, a Performance is often referred to as a "play." Performers usually participate in rehearsals beforehand to practice the work. An effective Performance is determined by the achieved skills and competency of the performer, also known as the level of skill and knowledge. In 1994, Spencer and McClelland defined competency as a combination of motives, traits, self-concepts, attitudes, cognitive behavior skills, and content knowledge that differentiates superior performers from average ones. Additionally, a Performance describes the way in which an actor performs, and in a solo capacity, it may refer to a mime artist, comedian, conjurer, magician, or other entertainer.

Another aspect of Performance that gained popularity in the early 20th century is performance art. Originating with Dada and Russian constructivism groups, this form focuses on avant-garde poetry readings and live paintings meant to be viewed by an audience. It can be scripted or completely improvised and may include audience participation. The emergence of abstract expressionism in the 1950s, with artists like Jackson Pollock and Willem de Kooning, led to action painting, a technique that emphasizes the dynamic movements of artists as they apply paint and other media to canvas or glass. For these artists, the motion of putting paint on canvas was as valuable as the finished painting, leading them to document their work in film, such as the short film "Jackson Pollock 51" (1951).

Situationists in France, led by Guy Debord, combined avant-garde art with revolutionary politics to incite everyday acts of anarchy. Their "Naked City Map" (1957) fragmented the 19 sections of Paris, using techniques like détournement and abstraction to deconstruct the geometry and order of a typical city map. At the New School for Social Research in New York, John Cage and Allan Kaprow developed happening performance art, which included the audience in chaotic and spontaneous acts. These happenings challenged traditional art conventions and encouraged artists to consider the role of the audience. In Japan, the 1954 Gutai group, led by Yoshihara Jiro, Kanayama Akira, Murakami Saburo, Kazuo Shiraga, and Shimamoto Shozo, brought the materials of art-making to life through body movement, blurring the lines between art and theater. Kazuo Shiraga's "Challenging Mud" (1955) exemplifies this approach, as he rolled and moved in mud, using his body as the art-making tool, emphasizing the temporary nature of performance art.
