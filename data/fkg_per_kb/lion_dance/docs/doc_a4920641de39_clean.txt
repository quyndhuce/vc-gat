The Lion Dance, a traditional Chinese performance, involves mimicking a lion's movements in a lion costume to bring good fortune and ward off evil. This dance is typically performed during the Lunar New Year and other significant cultural and religious festivals. Additionally, it is featured at business openings, special celebrations, and wedding ceremonies, often to honor distinguished guests within Chinese communities.

The Lion Dance is distinct from the Dragon Dance, which requires multiple performers holding a long dragon on poles. The Lion Dance usually involves two dancers, one controlling the lion's head and the other its tail. Accompanied by a vigorous drumbeat and the sounds of gongs and cymbals, the Lion Dance includes movements that are also found in Chinese martial arts.

In China, there are various forms of the Lion Dance, with the Northern Lion and Southern Lion being the most prominent. The Southern Lion, prevalent in Southeast Asia due to the historical migration of Southern Chinese communities, is particularly widespread. Variations of the Lion Dance, inspired by the Chinese version, are also practiced in Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, and Vietnam. Other forms of lion dancing exist in India, Indonesia, and East Africa.

The tradition of dancers wearing animal or mythical beast masks dates back to ancient China. References to such performances can be found in texts like the Shujing, where wild beasts and phoenixes are described as dancing. During the Qin and Han dynasties, dancers wore bear masks for exorcism rituals, and mime performers represented fish, dragons, and phoenixes.

Although lions are not native to China, the Lion Dance likely originated from countries like India or Persia and was introduced through Central Asia. The term "shi," meaning lion, might have been derived from the Persian word "šer." The earliest use of "shizi" to denote a lion appears in Han dynasty texts, with lions being gifted to the Han court by emissaries from Central Asia and the Parthian Empire.

Detailed descriptions of the Lion Dance emerged during the Tang dynasty, when it was recognized as a foreign dance by writers and poets. However, references to "lion acts" date back to the third century AD, as noted by a Three Kingdoms scholar, Meng Kang, in his commentary on Hanshu. Early in its history, the Lion Dance was linked to Buddhism, with records indicating that a lion led parades for Buddha statues to ward off evil spirits. This suggests that the dance could have evolved from a local tradition that adopted Buddhist symbolism.

During the Tang dynasty, several versions of the Lion Dance existed. At the Tang court, it was known as the "Great Peace Music" or the "Lion Dance of the Five Directions," featuring five lions of different colors and expressions, each controlled by two individuals and accompanied by 140 singers. Another version involved five lions over three meters tall, each with twelve assistants who teased the lions with red whisks. A poem by Tang poet Bai Juyi describes a Lion Dance performed by two Central Asian dancers wearing a wooden-headed, silk-tailed, and furry-bodied lion costume with golden eyes, silver teeth, and moving ears, similar to today’s Lion Dance. By the eighth century, this Lion Dance had reached Japan. During the Song dynasty, the Lion Dance became common in festivals and was known as the Northern Lion during the Southern Song period.

The Southern Lion, originating in Guangdong province, represents a later development. Several myths surround its origins, including one where villagers celebrated after driving away a mythical monster named Nian. Another legend attributes its creation to an auspicious dream experienced by the Qianlong Emperor.
