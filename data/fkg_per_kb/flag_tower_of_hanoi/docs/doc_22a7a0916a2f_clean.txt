The Flag Tower of Hanoi, known in Vietnamese as Cột cờ Hà Nội, stands as a monumental relic within the Thang Long Imperial Citadel in Ba Dinh District, Hanoi. Begun in 1805 and completed in 1812 during the reign of King Gia Long, this iconic structure was constructed alongside the citadel in the Vauban style, reflecting the architectural preferences of the Nguyen dynasty. Rising to a height of 60 meters, the tower is a testament to the grandeur and strategic importance of the citadel, serving both as a military watchtower and a symbol of national pride.

The tower's foundation is a multi-tiered truncated pyramid, occupying an expansive area of 2007 square meters. This base consists of three levels, each progressively narrower than the one below, creating a stable and visually balanced structure. Each level is adorned with decorative walls featuring intricate patterns, adding to the aesthetic appeal of the tower. Access to the upper levels is achieved by climbing 18 steps on the eastern and western sides, with additional steps leading to the third level, which houses four ornately named doors—Nghênh Húc (Welcoming the Morning Sun), Hồi Quang (Reflected Light), Hướng Minh (Facing the Light), and another door facing north. The third level, accessible via 14 steps, is crowned with a small pavilion where a prominent red flag waves against the sky, symbolizing the nation’s sovereignty and resilience.

Constructed primarily from brick, the tower’s exterior is characterized by its light yellow hue, which contrasts beautifully with the blue sky and lush greenery surrounding it. The cylindrical shaft of the tower, tapering slightly as it ascends, is punctuated by small windows that serve both as architectural details and as vents for the interior. These windows, evenly distributed around the shaft, allow natural light to filter into the tower, enhancing its internal atmosphere. The surface of the walls bears the marks of weathering, giving the tower an aged and authentic appearance that resonates with its historical significance.

The Flag Tower of Hanoi is not merely a physical structure; it is a living symbol of Hanoi’s rich history and cultural heritage. It stands as a testament to the city’s long-standing tradition of patriotism and serves as a focal point for national celebrations and commemorations. As a national relic of special significance, the tower is recognized by UNESCO as part of the broader heritage landscape of the Thang Long Imperial Citadel, further cementing its status as a site of international importance. Visitors to the tower can explore its various levels, gaining insight into the architectural ingenuity of the Nguyen dynasty and the enduring legacy of the Thang Long Citadel.
