Situated in the bustling heart of Hanoi's historic district, Hang Bac Street stretches for a length of 330 meters, nestled within the Hoan Kiem District. This vibrant thoroughfare is conveniently located near the Hoan Kiem Lake, allowing visitors to reach the iconic Ngoc Son Temple in just 10 to 15 minutes by walking northward. The street is bordered by other notable streets such as Hang Mam, Hang Be, and Pho Phat Lieu, while also touching upon Hang Gai and Hang Dao to the west. It intersects with Ngang Street at Ma May and Dong Xuan Market at Tho Mo Lane, creating a lively network of commerce and culture.

The history of Hang Bac Street dates back to the late 19th century during French colonial rule when it was known as Rue des changeurs, meaning the street of money changers. After the August Revolution in 1945, the street was renamed Hang Bac, which translates to Silver Street. According to historical records, the Emperor Le Thanh Tong, in the 15th century, granted permission to the village of Chau Khe (or Trau Khe), located in Thuong Khanh Commune, Binh Giang District, Hai Duong Province, to produce silverware and coins. This village was then relocated to Hang Bac Street, where they established a workshop at number 58 Hang Bac. The historical marker at number 42 Hang Bac further corroborates this tradition, indicating that the street has been home to silver workshops since the time of the Le Dynasty or even earlier.

The craft of silver making on Hang Bac Street has a storied past, originating from the village of Chau Khe, which was renowned for its skilled artisans who produced exquisite silverware. During the reign of King Le Thanh Tong in the 15th century, the village was tasked with producing silverware and coins for the royal court in Thang Long, now modern-day Hanoi. When the Nguyen Dynasty moved their capital to Hue in the early 19th century, they brought along the tradition of silver making, ensuring its continuity. Today, descendants of the Chau Khe community continue to practice this ancient craft, contributing to the vibrant atmosphere of Hang Bac Street.

Throughout the centuries, the street has evolved into a hub of activity, not only for the original inhabitants but also for those who have settled there more recently. The residents of Hang Bac Street maintain strong ties with their ancestral roots, engaging in various aspects of life including business, social activities, and cultural practices. Despite the passage of time, the spirit of the village remains alive, with many families still participating in communal activities and festivals, fostering a sense of unity and shared heritage.

Visitors to Hang Bac Street can explore numerous historical sites and landmarks that reflect the rich cultural heritage of the area. Notable among these is the Trang Xuong Silver Workshop at number 58, which continues to produce fine silverware. The Thien Thang Pagoda at number 50 and the Dong Han Stele at number 42 are also significant, with the latter dating back to the late 18th century and featuring intricate carvings that tell the story of the street’s history. The Kim Ngan Pagoda, with its striking red lacquer finish, stands out as one of the most prominent structures in the area, adding to the visual appeal of the street.

Hang Bac Street is a living testament to the enduring legacy of traditional craftsmanship and cultural significance. The architecture of the buildings along the street reflects the historical layers of its development, with many retaining the traditional Vietnamese architectural style characterized by ornate facades and intricate wood carvings. The narrow lanes and closely packed houses create a cozy and intimate atmosphere, inviting visitors to immerse themselves in the daily life of the community. The street is a vibrant blend of old-world charm and contemporary energy, offering a unique glimpse into the rich tapestry of Hanoi’s cultural heritage.

As visitors stroll down Hang Bac Street, they are greeted by the sounds of hammering and the clinking of silverware being crafted, the aroma of freshly baked pastries wafting from local bakeries, and the sight of artisans meticulously working on their crafts. The street is a dynamic space where tradition meets modernity, providing a fascinating insight into the lives of the people who call Hang Bac Street home. Whether it’s the intricate silver jewelry, the bustling markets, or the warm hospitality of the locals, Hang Bac Street offers a memorable experience that captures the essence of Hanoi’s rich cultural heritage.

Hang Bac Street, located in Hoan Kiem District of Hanoi, is a historic thoroughfare that stretches for 280 meters and is 6.5 meters wide. The street runs from the end of Hang Mam Street at the intersection with Ma May Street, crossing through the junction with Ta Hien and Dinh Lieu streets, and terminates where it meets the boundary between Hang Ngang and Hang Dao streets. This narrow lane has been a significant part of Hanoi's rich history, dating back to the time of the Le Dynasty when it was known as Dong Cac ward, which served as the setting for the story "Mieu lua" in Vu trung tuy bieu by Pham Dinh Ho. During this period, Dong Cac was also the site of silver ingot exchange and trade, a practice that continued into the 19th century when the area was part of Dong Tho and Dung Han villages within the Huu Tuc district of Tho Xuan county.

Under French colonial rule, the street was referred to as Rue des Changeurs, reflecting its role as a center for silver exchange. The current name, Hang Bac, was officially adopted after the revolution. Today, Hang Bac Street falls under the jurisdiction of Hang Bac ward, Hoan Kiem district.

The street's history is deeply intertwined with three distinct trades: silver ingot casting, jewelry making, and money exchange. The craft of silver ingot casting was brought to Hang Bac by residents of Truong Khe village, now part of Binh Giang district in Hai Duong province. According to legend, during the reign of King Le Thanh Tong (1460-1497), a man named Luu Xuan Tin from Truong Khe village was appointed as the head of the Upper Treasury Office and received permission from the king to establish a silver ingot casting workshop in the capital. He then brought villagers from his home to work in this specialized trade. The village of Truong Khe had five clans, each represented by different generations who moved to Hang Bac to engage in the silver casting business. These clans established two communal houses to honor their ancestors and the profession: the upper communal house, or Truong communal house (number 50), and the lower communal house, or Kim Ngan communal house (number 42).

The communal houses were not only places of worship but also served as centers for processing silver ingots. The term "trang," meaning workshop, refers to the place where silver was melted and cast into ingots. The "ding," or communal house, was responsible for receiving raw materials and delivering finished products to the official office, known as the Ty quan, who acted on behalf of the royal court. The silver casting trade was exclusive to the people of Truong Khe-Hang Bac due to the need to maintain secrecy and prevent fraud, given that silver ingots were used as currency in ancient times. However, the communal houses did not honor Luu Xuan Tin but rather worshipped Hien Van, a mythical figure considered the ancestor of all crafts.

By the late 19th century, the population of Truong Khe residents in Hang Bac had grown significantly, necessitating the acquisition of additional space for communal activities. They purchased the Inner Temple (Nội Miếu) of the Hài Tượng shoemakers' community to create a memorial hall called Truong Khe Vong So, dedicated to the spirits of those who had returned to their ancestral homes.

In addition to silver casting, the residents of Truong Khe also engaged in money exchange, converting large sums of money from coins and small pieces of silver into more manageable forms. This service was essential before the advent of paper currency, as handling large amounts of metal coins or small silver pieces was cumbersome and inconvenient.

The silver casting industry ceased operations at the beginning of the 19th century when Emperor Gia Long moved the capital to Hue, compelling the silver casting workshops to relocate there. However, the money exchange business continued until the arrival of the French colonizers.

Hang Bac Street also hosted another important trade related to silver and gold: jewelry making. This craft encompassed three sub-disciplines: engraving, which involved carving designs onto gold and silver objects; drawing, which entailed pulling molten gold or silver into fine threads and weaving them into intricate patterns; and polishing, which focused on creating smooth, shiny surfaces without any embellishments.

Jewelry makers from Ding Cong Thượng, now part of Hoang Mai district in Hanoi, were known for their skills in these areas. Legend has it that during the Ly Nam De era (6th century), three brothers from Ding Cong Thượng—Tran Dian, Tran Dien, and Tran Hoa—learned the art of jewelry making and taught it to their fellow villagers. These artisans settled in Hang Bac but initially resided at the eastern end of Hang Bo street. They built a communal house in Hang Bo to honor their ancestors, with the altar later relocated to the communal house in Ding Cong Thượng.

Another group of jewelry makers came from Dong Sam village in Kien Xuong district, Thai Binh province. While both groups practiced engraving, drawing, and polishing, they often specialized in different types of jewelry. Dong Sam craftsmen were known for creating larger silver items such as incense burners, boxes, trays, and bowls, while Ding Cong artisans focused on smaller, ornamental pieces like earrings, necklaces, and rings.

Until the early 20th century, Hang Bac retained its status as a traditional street, characterized by its rows of "diem" houses, a type of Vietnamese architecture featuring high, narrow structures with overhanging second floors. The shops along the street were simple yet functional, equipped with basic tools such as molds, anvils, and polishing stones.

The street also featured two communal houses representing the original inhabitants of Dong Cac, the former Dong Han and Dong Tho villages. These communal houses, located at number 54 and 24 respectively, served as centers for local traditions and rituals.
