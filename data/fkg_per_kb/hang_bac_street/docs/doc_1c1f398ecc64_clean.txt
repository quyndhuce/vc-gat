Hang Bac Street is located within the ancient quarter of Hanoi. During the late 19th century under French colonial rule, it was known as Rue des changeurs (Street of Money Exchangers). In 1945, the street was renamed Hang Bac.

Previously, Hang Bac Street belonged to Phuong Hang Bac, Hoan Kiem District, but now it is part of Hoan Kiem Ward, Hanoi City. The street stretches approximately 0.5 kilometers from east to northeast. At its western end, it intersects with Hang Ngang, Hang Dao, and Hang Bo streets, while its eastern end meets Hang Mam Street. It is situated about 0.3 kilometers north of Hoan Kiem Lake.

According to historical records inscribed on a stele at the Dong Han Temple (number 42), Hang Bac Street was established during the Le Dynasty or slightly earlier. During this period, Hang Bac was part of Dong Cac ward, Tho Xuan county. Under the Nguyen Dynasty, the area belonged to Dong Tho village, Tho Xuan county, Hoai Duc prefecture, Hanoi province.

The traditional jewelry craft on Hang Bac Street has roots in the village of Chau Khe in Thuc Khang commune, Binh Giang district, Hai Duong province. In the 15th century, during the reign of King Le Thanh Tong, Shangshu Liu Xuan Tin, originally from Chau Khe village, was tasked by the court to establish a silver minting workshop in the capital city of Thang Long (now Hanoi). By the early 19th century, when the Nguyen dynasty moved to Hue, they took the silver minting workshop with them. However, most of the Chau Khe craftsmen remained in Thang Long and continued their traditional jewelry trade, establishing a guild in what is now Hang Bac Street. Additionally, Hang Bac attracted goldsmiths from Ding Cong and Dong Tam to settle there.

Throughout its development, the community of Hang Bac craftsmen, including those from Chau Khe, maintained strong bonds, supporting each other in their professions, businesses, and cultural and religious activities. Despite living in the city, they retained a sense of village life, returning annually on the 19th day of the first lunar month to participate in the village festival, offering prayers to the Village Guardian and honoring the Ancestral Craftsmen.

Today, Hang Bac Street still features several houses that bear witness to the history of the Chau Khe craftsmen. For instance, number 58 was once a silver minting workshop, number 50 houses the Upper Shrine, and number 42 houses the Lower Shrine, which were sites for collecting and processing silver ingots. The Dong Han Temple (number 42), built at the end of the 18th century during the 44th year of the Canh Hung era, is dedicated to Ling Lang Wang and also houses a shrine for a female deity. This temple is the largest in the old quarter of Hanoi.

Since the reign of Emperor Gia Long, Hang Bac Street had an official responsible for managing silver transactions, collecting scrap silver from various provinces, and delivering it to the minting workshops. After refining the silver into ingots, these would be returned to the provinces and then sent to the capital to be deposited into the treasury.

Using traditional handcraft techniques, the artisans of Hang Bac have produced exquisite jewelry such as rings, earrings, bracelets, and necklaces for women and children.
