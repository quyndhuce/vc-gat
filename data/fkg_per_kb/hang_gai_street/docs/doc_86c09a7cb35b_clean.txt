Hang Gai Street, located in Hoan Kiem District of Hanoi, is a historic thoroughfare that stretches for 273 meters and is 11 meters wide. It begins at the intersection near Dong Kinh Nghia Thuc Square, adjacent to Hang Dao Street, and extends to the junction of Hang Hom and Hang Trong streets, where it connects with Hang Bong Street and intersects with Luong Van Can Street. This street has a rich history dating back to before the French colonial era, when it was known as "rue du Chanvre" by the French, and later reclaimed its original Vietnamese name, Hang Gai, in 1945. The alternative local name for this street is Hang Thung, reflecting its historical significance in selling hemp products such as ropes, nets, and other related goods.

The origins of Hang Gai Street can be traced back to two ancient neighborhoods: Dong Ha and Co Vu, both part of the former Tho Xuong district. These areas were home to two ancient communal houses, the Dong Ha Temple at number 46 and the Co Vu Temple at number 85, which are now sadly converted into private residences and a kindergarten respectively. The Dong Ha Temple honors Quy Minh, a brother of Son Tinh who helped fend off the floodwaters sent by Thuy Tinh, while the Co Vu Temple venerates Bai Ma and Ling Lang, figures associated with the nearby Hang Buom and Cau Giay districts.

Historically, Hang Gai Street was a hub for the production and sale of hemp-related goods, but from the 19th century onwards, the printing industry began to flourish here. Printers and book sellers established numerous shops along the street, gradually pushing the hemp traders to move to Dong Thanh and Bat Dan streets. During the lead-up to the Mid-Autumn Festival each year, Hang Gai Street transforms into a bustling market for traditional children's toys, lanterns, and paper-cutting displays.

The northern end of Hang Gai Street, from its junction with Hang Dao Street to To Tich Street, was formerly known as Hang Tien, a neighborhood where skilled craftsmen from Nhi Khê village sold intricately carved wooden items like trays, wine stands, incense tubes, window frames, round tables, and small wooden toys. Further down the street, the area became renowned for its printing presses and bookstores. Notable among these were the Quan Van Duong and Tu Van Duong establishments, which were significant publishers of books during the late 19th century. These publishers produced a wide range of literature, including Confucian classics, romantic novels, and historical texts, using the traditional method of woodblock printing.

During the French colonial period, Hang Gai Street saw the emergence of modern printing presses that used the Latin script, alongside traditional woodblock printing. Prominent among these were the Dong Kinh Press at number 82 and the Ngo Tu Ha Press at number 104. Additionally, the first French-run general store opened on the site of the former governor's residence, marking the beginning of a new era of commercial activity. Over time, various other shops selling miscellaneous goods, hat stores, and optical shops sprouted up, contributing to the vibrant commercial atmosphere of the street.

In the early days of the national resistance war against the French, Hang Gai Street served as the southern boundary of the First Liberation Zone. During this period, the street was a frontline battleground, with the odd-numbered side controlled by the enemy and the even-numbered side serving as the guerrilla line. Historical accounts, such as those detailed in "Hanoi 60 Days of Smoke and Fire" by Vuong Thu Vu and "The Capital Division" by Duy Duc, highlight the bravery of local residents like Luong Vy, a high school student who led the self-defense team of Hang Gai Street. For 15 consecutive days, he infiltrated enemy lines to carry out ambushes, earning him one of the earliest awards of the War Medal from the First Liberation Zone.

Today, Hang Gai Street retains much of its historical charm, offering visitors a glimpse into the rich cultural heritage of Hanoi. The street's architecture, though influenced by colonial-era buildings, still preserves elements of traditional Vietnamese design, blending seamlessly with the modern urban landscape. Visitors can explore the narrow lanes lined with old houses, many of which have been converted into cafes, boutiques, and art galleries, providing a unique blend of past and present. The atmosphere is lively, with the sounds of street vendors, the chatter of tourists, and the occasional clatter of bicycles creating a dynamic scene that reflects the city's enduring spirit.

Hang Gai Street, nestled in the heart of Hanoi's Old Quarter, is a vibrant tapestry woven with threads of history, culture, and commerce. Stretching for approximately 250 meters, this bustling thoroughfare connects seamlessly to other historic streets such as Hang Dao, Le Thai To, and Hang Hoam, creating a network that encapsulates the essence of Hanoi’s ancient charm. The street’s eastern end meets the bustling intersection of Hang Dao and Le Thai To, while its western side leads to Hang Hoam and Hang Trong, eventually connecting to Hang Bong Street. In between, it intersects with Luan Van Can and To Tich Streets, forming a complex yet harmonious urban layout.

Historically, Hang Gai Street was known for selling rope and rattan products, hence its original name derived from the Vietnamese term "gai," meaning rattan. During the French colonial era, the street was referred to as "rue de Chanvre" or "rue de Gai," reflecting the same trade in French. Over time, the street evolved into a hub for silk and brocade, particularly after the 19th century when the printing industry began to flourish here. This shift marked a significant transformation, as silk merchants gradually replaced the earlier vendors of rope and rattan goods. Today, Hang Gai Street is renowned as one of the premier centers for silk trading in Hanoi, with over a hundred shops dedicated to the sale and production of silk products.

The architecture of Hang Gai Street is a blend of old and new, reflecting the city’s dynamic evolution. Traditional houses with their characteristic wooden doors and lattice windows still stand alongside modern structures, each contributing to the street’s unique aesthetic. Many of these traditional homes feature small gardens and courtyards, offering a glimpse into the past. Some houses retain their original design, with rear entrances leading to narrow alleys that connect to other streets, preserving the intricate web of pathways that define the Old Quarter. These alleys, often no wider than a few dozen centimeters, are reminiscent of the tunnels found in Cu Chi, evoking a sense of nostalgia and historical depth.

The cultural significance of Hang Gai Street extends beyond its commercial activities. It is home to several historical sites, including the ancient Cau Vu Temple at number 85, which honors the White Horse and Ling Lang, accompanied by a centuries-old banyan tree. This temple serves as a testament to the area’s rich heritage and spiritual traditions. Additionally, the street is dotted with remnants of its past, such as the former photography studio Tam Anh, the Nui Dien café, and the Hien Thuc Cooperative for engraving seals, all of which contribute to the street’s storied identity.

Visitors to Hang Gai Street can immerse themselves in a world where tradition and modernity coexist harmoniously. The street is lined with shops selling silk and brocade, many of which source their materials from the famous Vạn Phúc Silk Village. The atmosphere is lively and vibrant, with a constant stream of tourists and locals browsing through the colorful displays of silk fabrics, scarves, and garments. The shops not only sell finished products but also offer workshops where visitors can watch artisans at work, learning about the meticulous processes involved in weaving and dyeing silk. This hands-on experience allows visitors to appreciate the skill and artistry behind each piece of fabric.

During the warmer months, especially autumn, Hang Gai Street takes on a special allure. The mild weather and the scent of frangipani flowers create a serene ambiance, enhanced by the sight of vendors selling fresh young rice cakes and the golden leaves falling from the trees. The street becomes a picturesque setting, perfect for leisurely strolls and photo opportunities. Visitors can explore the narrow lanes and hidden corners, discovering hidden gems and experiencing the authentic charm of Hanoi’s Old Quarter.

Travelers can reach Hang Gai Street via various means of transportation, including bus, taxi, or motorbike. Walking through the streets provides a more intimate experience, allowing one to fully absorb the unique character of the area. From the bustling intersections to the quiet alleyways, every step reveals a story of Hanoi’s rich history and enduring cultural legacy. Whether visiting during the day or evening, Hang Gai Street offers a captivating journey through time, where the past and present converge in a symphony of colors, sounds, and scents.
