Tay Ho Temple, nestled on a large island in the middle of Tay Ho Lake, is one of the most revered spiritual sites in Hanoi, attracting numerous tourists from both domestic and international visitors each year. This temple, dedicated to Princess Liêu Hành, holds significant historical and cultural importance within Vietnamese beliefs alongside other prominent deities such as the Jade Emperor, Saint Giong, and Saint Tien.

According to legend, Princess Liêu Hành was the second daughter of Emperor Hoang Vu, who was exiled due to her involvement in a love affair that violated royal norms. After being banished to the shores of Tay Ho Lake, she helped the local people ward off evil spirits, ensure peace, prosperity, and tranquility, thus becoming a revered figure among the community.

The exact date of the temple's construction remains uncertain, though it is believed to have been built sometime during the 17th century, possibly even earlier. Historical records do not provide clear documentation of its existence until the late 20th century when it was recognized as a historical and cultural site by the Ministry of Culture, Sports, and Tourism in 1996.

Upon entering Tay Ho Temple, visitors are greeted by a grand entrance adorned with intricate carvings and inscriptions. The main hall, constructed in the traditional Chinese architectural style, features a three-entrance gate known as the "Three Holy Gates." The temple's main structure is a two-story building with a roof bearing the inscription "Tay Ho Historical Site" in elegant Han script. The central part of the hall is marked by a red-lacquered ceiling, while the side sections feature blue-and-white porcelain tiles, symbolizing the harmony and balance of nature.

Inside the temple, the architecture is a testament to traditional craftsmanship, with detailed wood carvings and ceramic decorations adorning every corner. The main hall houses three statues representing the Three Goddesses: Lady Tran Vuong, Lady Thoi (the Fairy), and Lady Duong (the Dragon). Each statue is dressed in vibrant costumes that reflect their respective roles and attributes. Lady Tran Vuong wears a green robe symbolizing growth and vitality, Lady Thoi dons a white robe signifying purity and clarity, and Lady Duong is attired in a yellow robe representing warmth and light. These figures are revered for their benevolent powers and are believed to bestow blessings upon worshippers, ensuring a prosperous and harmonious life.

Adjacent to the main hall is the Shrine of the Gods, a three-story pagoda with eight curved roofs. Inside, the shrine is divided into two sections, with the upper floor dedicated to the worship of the God of Wealth and the lower floor housing the Three Fairies. The temple grounds also include a guesthouse and accommodation facilities for pilgrims and tourists.

In addition to the main structures, the temple grounds are home to a sacred tree known as the "Vietnamese Sacred Tree," which is said to possess mystical powers. Legend has it that this tree was planted by Princess Liêu Hành herself, and its presence adds to the spiritual ambiance of the temple. Nearby, there is also a golden turtle statue, a symbol of longevity and good fortune, which further enhances the temple's cultural significance.

Visiting Tay Ho Temple offers a unique blend of historical exploration and spiritual reflection. The serene setting of the temple, surrounded by the tranquil waters of Tay Ho Lake, provides a peaceful atmosphere conducive to meditation and contemplation. Visitors can wander through the beautifully landscaped gardens, admire the intricate details of the temple's architecture, and participate in traditional rituals and ceremonies.

For those interested in exploring more of Hanoi's cultural heritage, the area around Tay Ho Temple is rich with other notable attractions. Nearby, one can visit Tran Quoc Pagoda, the picturesque Tay Ho Lake, Quan Thanh Temple, Van Mieng - National Village for Handicrafts and Ceramics, Lang Church, and the Lotus Pond of Ho Chi Minh. Each of these sites offers a glimpse into the rich tapestry of Hanoi's history and culture, making the region a must-visit destination for anyone seeking a deeper understanding of Vietnam's spiritual and artistic traditions.

Nestled within the heart of Hanoi, Tay Ho Temple stands as a revered spiritual destination that captivates both locals and tourists alike. This ancient site, located at the picturesque shores of West Lake, is not only a testament to the rich cultural heritage of Vietnam but also a place steeped in legend and history. Established as a significant historical and cultural landmark, Tay Ho Temple was officially recognized by the Ministry of Culture, Sports, and Tourism on February 13, 1996, cementing its importance in the annals of Vietnamese culture.

The temple's location on the expansive peninsula of Nghi Tam village, jutting out into the serene waters of West Lake, provides it with a tranquil and enchanting setting. Situated approximately 4 kilometers west of the bustling city center, Tay Ho Temple offers visitors a peaceful retreat from the urban hustle and bustle. The temple's opening hours are generally from 5:00 AM to 7:00 PM, though during the two major festivals held on the 3rd and 18th days of the third lunar month, the temple remains open longer due to the influx of pilgrims and tourists.

Tay Ho Temple is dedicated to the worship of Lady Lieu Hanh, one of the Four Immortals in Vietnamese folk religion. According to local lore, Lady Lieu Hanh is believed to be the second princess of Jade Emperor who was banished to earth for breaking a jade cup. Upon descending to the mortal realm, she wandered extensively, eventually settling by the scenic West Lake. Here, she operated a tea stall, using it as a pretext to indulge in literary pursuits while simultaneously aiding the local populace and vanquishing evil spirits. Her benevolent deeds earned her the title of "Mother of Heaven" during the Nguyen dynasty.

One of the most captivating legends associated with Tay Ho Temple involves the encounter between Lady Lieu Hanh and Trang Bung Phung Khac Khoan, a renowned scholar and former prime minister. It is said that during one of his excursions around West Lake, Phung Khac Khoan visited Lady Lieu Hanh's tea stall. The two shared a profound connection, culminating in the composition of the famous poem "Tay Ho Imperial Tea House." After Lady Lieu Hanh's departure, Phung Khac Khoan erected a shrine at the site to honor their meeting, which eventually evolved into the grand structure known today as Tay Ho Temple.

The architectural splendor of Tay Ho Temple is a harmonious blend of traditional Vietnamese craftsmanship and spiritual symbolism. As visitors approach the temple, they are greeted by the imposing presence of the two-tiered gatehouse, adorned with intricate carvings that reflect the unique cultural heritage of Vietnam. Beyond the entrance, the main hall, or Phu, serves as the central sanctuary where devotees pay homage to the Three Holy Mothers. The rear chamber, known as the Hau cung, houses the revered statues of the Three Holy Mothers—Lady Thang Ngan, Lady Lieu Hanh, and Lady Thoai—each distinguished by their distinct attire and head coverings.

Adjacent to the main hall are the separate shrines dedicated to Lady Thang Ngan and her twelve mountain attendants, collectively known as the Mountain Ladies. These shrines are complemented by the imposing figures of the Five Tigers, symbolic guardians that embody strength, courage, and the power to ward off evil. The reverence for these tiger deities underscores the deep-rooted belief in supernatural protection, integral to the worship of Mother Goddesses in Vietnamese culture.

The temple grounds also feature the Lầu Cô and Lầu Cậu pavilions, situated on either side of the courtyard. These structures honor the female and male attendants who serve the deities within the temple, adding layers of complexity to the spiritual landscape and reflecting the community's respect for both divine and human servants.

The cultural significance of Tay Ho Temple extends far beyond its physical structure, encompassing a vibrant tradition of worship and celebration. Central to this tradition is the veneration of Lady Lieu Hanh, a figure deeply embedded in the pantheon of Mother Goddesses worshipped across Vietnam. The annual festival held on the 3rd day of the third lunar month marks the anniversary of Lady Lieu Hanh's death and draws thousands of devotees, residents, and visitors to participate in a series of solemn rituals and mesmerizing performances of ca tru, a traditional form of ceremonial singing. These performances serve as a conduit for communication with the divine, expressing devotion and supplication, and contribute to the temple's atmosphere of reverence and tranquility.

In summary, Tay Ho Temple is more than just a place of worship; it is a living museum of Vietnamese spirituality, history, and artistry. Its architecture, legends, and cultural practices offer a window into the soul of Vietnam, inviting visitors to immerse themselves in a world where the past and present converge in a tapestry of faith and tradition.
