Hang Ruoi Street is a small ancient street located in Hoan Kiem district, Hanoi, stretching approximately 108 meters long and 6 meters wide. The street runs from the intersection with Hang Loc Street to Hang Ma Street. Historically, this area was a part of the western embankment of the ancient To Lich River, which facilitated the docking of boats and the handling of aquatic products. It was situated within the old administrative boundaries of the villages of Vinh Tru and An Phu (later renamed to Yen Phu), belonging to the Hau Tuc ward of the former Tho Xuan district.

During the lunar month of September each year, when the rươi (a type of soft-bodied worm) breeding season occurs in brackish water areas, farmers from regions such as Hai Duong, Hai Phong, Nam Dinh, and Thai Binh would row their boats up the Red River, enter the To Lich River, and set up temporary stalls along the riverbank to form a bustling wholesale market for rươi. This seasonal market attracted traders who distributed the rươi to various markets and alleys within the imperial city of Thang Long to meet the unique culinary demands of Hanoi residents, including dishes like rươi paste, steamed rươi with gac leaves, or braised rươi. Consequently, the street acquired its name "Hang Rươi."

Under French colonial rule at the end of the 19th century, the street was officially named Rue des Vers Blancs, translating the local term for rươi into French. During this period, the system of ponds and parts of the To Lich River were gradually filled in to create standardized roads. After the August Revolution in 1945, the Vietnamese government reinstated the original Vietnamese name "Hang Rươi," which has remained unchanged until today.

Despite its short length, Hang Ruoi Street features two significant historical landmarks: the ancient Yen Phu communal house at number 17, which serves as evidence of the long-standing settlement history of the area, and the revolutionary site at number 4, which commemorates important events during the resistance war in the capital.

The street's historical significance extends beyond its current function, as it no longer primarily serves as a marketplace for rươi. Today, fewer households engage in preparing rươi-based dishes, having shifted to other occupations that offer higher income.
