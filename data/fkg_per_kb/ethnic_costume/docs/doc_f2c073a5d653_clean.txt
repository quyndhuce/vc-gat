Polish Ethnic Costume is highly diverse and distinctive across various regions. Typically, people do not wear these traditional outfits daily but reserve them for special occasions such as folk festivals, traditional weddings, religious holidays, harvest celebrations, and other significant events. The attire can reflect regional identity and even marital status.

These ethnic costumes are found throughout Poland, including regions like Wielkopolska, Małopolska, Mazovia, Pomerania, Warmia, Masuria, Podlasie, Kujawy, and Silesia. In Małopolska, particularly around Kraków, women's costumes consist of white blouses with embroidered and beaded vests, floral-patterned skirts, aprons, coral bead necklaces, and laced-up shoes. Unmarried girls may wear wreaths with ribbons, while married women often wear white headscarves. Men typically wear blue waistcoats with embroidery and fringes, striped trousers, and decorated Krakuska hats adorned with ribbons and peacock feathers, along with metal buckles on their belts.

The Lachy Sądeckie ethnic costume is prevalent in southern Małopolska, especially in the Nowosądecki district and the Kotlina Sądecka region. The Górale costume, found in southern Poland along the Carpathian Mountains, in Podhale within the Tatra range, and parts of the Beskids, varies by location. In Podkarpacie, the highland Polish ethnic costume is prominent, particularly in Rzeszów, among the Lasowiacy people. The Lublin region features the traditional Krzczonów ethnic costume, seen in areas like Biłgoraj.

In Silesia, Bytom and Piekary Śląskie in Upper Silesia, as well as Těšínské Slezsko and Lower Silesia, distinct ethnic costumes are worn. In the regions of Pomorze, Kujawy, and Warmia, the Kashubian ethnic costume is common, especially in Kashubia, located in northern central Poland. Other traditional costumes are scattered throughout Mazowsze, appearing in Łowicz, Wilanów, Opoczno, and Sieradz. Podlasie also has its own unique ethnic costume.

In Wielkopolska, Bambrzy and Szamotuly showcase their traditional attire, alongside Biskupianski. The Szlachta, the Polish noble class, have their own traditional clothing, including the kontusz, pas kontuszowy (sash), and red żupan. In the 18th century, the czamara was popular, especially in Kraków, and by the 19th century, it became a symbol of national pride in Poland.
