Hang Dieu street, located in Hoan Kiem district of Hanoi, is a historic thoroughfare that stretches for 286 meters and is 8 meters wide. It connects from Bat Dan street to Duong Thanh street, bordering Hang Da market. The street's origins trace back to the land of the ancient village of Vien Noi, part of the former Tho Xuan county, which was later renamed to Thuong My. This area originally comprised two villages, Yen Noi Cu Vu, corresponding to the Hang Da area, and Yen Noi Dong Thanh, which encompasses Hang Dieu and Hang Non streets.

The street has a history dating back before the French colonial era, during which it was known as Rue des Pipes, meaning "Street of Pipes." After the August Revolution in 1945, it reclaimed its original Vietnamese name, Hang Dieu, which it has retained through subsequent name changes. Today, Hang Dieu falls under the administrative jurisdiction of Cua Dong ward.

In earlier times, Hang Dieu was renowned for selling various types of pipes used for smoking opium, such as silver-tipped and gold-tipped pipes, as well as clay pipes. However, by the early 19th century, the number of shops selling these items had dwindled, with only a few remaining to sell pipe bowls and silver tips. These items were also available in nearby streets like Bat Dan and Bat Su. Over time, the focus shifted towards leather goods, with shops specializing in making and selling leather products becoming predominant.

Distinct from the leather goods made in Ha Trung street, which included horse saddles, gun holsters, and schoolbags using hard western leather, Hang Dieu specialized in crafting soft Vietnamese leather footwear and accessories. Initially, they produced simple leather sandals and suede shoes, but later expanded into manufacturing European-style shoes and footwear using both domestic and imported leather from the Thuy Khe tannery. The artisans who established these shops were originally from the Truc Lam villages, specifically from Hang Giay and Hai Tang alley, moving to Hang Dieu to set up their businesses.

Midway along the street, at number 30, stands a temple dedicated to the Fire God, also known as the House Fire Temple. This temple was built in the 18th year of the reign of Emperor Minh Mang, which corresponds to 1838. The temple houses a large bell that would be rung whenever there was a fire nearby, serving both as a call for divine assistance and as an alarm to alert the community.

The cultural significance of Hang Dieu extends beyond its commercial activities. It represents a blend of traditional crafts and modern adaptations, reflecting the dynamic nature of Hanoi’s urban landscape. Visitors can explore the narrow lanes lined with old buildings, each telling a story of the city’s past. The atmosphere is one of quiet charm, with the sounds of leather being crafted and the sight of skilled artisans at work. This street not only offers a glimpse into the traditional crafts of Vietnam but also showcases the resilience and adaptability of local businesses in the face of changing times.

Visitors to Hang Dieu street will find themselves immersed in a world where the past meets the present, where the echoes of historical craftsmanship resonate alongside the buzz of contemporary commerce. The street remains a vital part of Hanoi’s cultural tapestry, preserving traditions while embracing innovation.

Historically, this street was part of the ancient Tran Thi Thuy ward, which later became known as the Thuong Tin ward during the Tran Dynasty, and eventually transformed into the Thuong Thinh ward under the Le Dynasty. Today, it falls under the administrative jurisdiction of Dong Xuan Ward, Hoan Kiem District.

In its early days, Hang Dieu street was renowned for selling various types of pipes, including those made of bamboo, wood, and other materials. The street's original purpose was to cater to the pipe-smoking culture prevalent among the local populace, particularly the habit of smoking tobacco through pipes. Over time, the street evolved to become a hub for artisans who specialized in crafting and selling pipes, as well as leather goods. The transition from pipe-making to leather goods production reflects the changing economic and social dynamics of the area.

The street's transformation into a center for leather goods began in the early 20th century when the local population shifted their focus from pipe-making to producing shoes and sandals. Initially, these were simple leather sandals and shoes, such as the traditional flat-bottomed sandals and leather-soled shoes. As time progressed, the craftsmen began to produce more sophisticated footwear inspired by Western styles, using both local and imported materials. This shift was influenced by the influx of ethnic Cham people from nearby Hang Giay and Hai Thuyen streets, who brought with them their expertise in leatherwork.

Today, while Hang Dieu street still retains some remnants of its past, most of the shops now sell leather goods rather than pipes. The street is home to numerous workshops and stores that specialize in leather products, ranging from shoes and sandals to bags and belts. Unlike Hang Trung street, which focuses on making wallets, purses, and other leather accessories, Hang Dieu street is dedicated to producing shoes and sandals crafted from local materials. This distinction highlights the unique character and identity of each street within the historic quarter of Hanoi.
