Hang Dieu street is located in the Old Quarter of Hanoi, specifically within Hoan Kiem district, stretching from Hang Ga street to Duong Than street, with a length of 276 meters. Historically, this area was part of the Yen Noi village in the former Tien Tuc commune, which later became the Thuong My commune of the old Tho Xuan county. Today, it falls under the Cua Dong ward of Hoan Kiem district.

In the past, Hang Dieu street was known for selling various types of pipes used for smoking opium, such as silver-tipped, gold-tipped pipes, bowl-shaped pipes, and clay pipes. However, by the early 20th century, only a few shops remained that sold these pipes, while most shops shifted to producing and selling leather goods. Unlike Hang Trung street, which specialized in making horse saddles, schoolbags, and gun holsters using Western leather, Hang Dieu focused on crafting shoes and sandals using local leather, initially producing simple sandals and suede shoes before moving on to European-style footwear made from Western leather.

Midway along the street, at number 30, stands the Fire God Temple, also known as the House Fire Temple. This temple venerates the Five Manifestations of Firelight Great Emperor, a deity believed to have the power to prevent fires. The temple was built in 1838 and houses a large bell that would ring during fires to both pray for divine assistance and serve as an alarm. Since its establishment in the 19th year of the Minh Mang era (1838), the Fire God Temple has become a significant religious center for worship and seeking blessings to avoid disasters, not only for residents of Hanoi but also for people across the country. In 1996, the temple was recognized as a historical and cultural relic. To commemorate the millennium anniversary of Thang Long-Hanoi, the Hoan Kiem District People's Committee initiated a major renovation project for 26 ancient relics in the district, including the Fire God Temple.

During the French colonial period, the street was called Rue des Pipes, and it reverted to its original Vietnamese name, Hang Dieu street, in 1945. Traditionally, in addition to betel chewing, smoking opium was also a social custom where pipes were offered to guests. As folk songs and oral traditions suggest:

"My body is like a pipe of opium,

You bury one pipe down and dig another up."

Or,

"Men smoke their pipes, women get dizzy,

A child lights a pipe and rolls out of the house,

An old man passes by,

Sniffs the smoke and gets dizzy for three or four days,..."

The bus routes that run through Hang Dieu street include lines 01 and 36, terminating at the end of the street.
