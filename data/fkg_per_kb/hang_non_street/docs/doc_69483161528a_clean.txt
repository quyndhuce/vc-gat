Hang Non Street, located in Hoan Kiem District of Hanoi, is a vibrant thoroughfare that stretches for 216 meters and spans a width of 6 meters. This historic street connects from Hang Quat Street to Duong Thanh Street, intersecting with Hang Dieu Street. The origins of Hang Non Street date back to the time before French colonial rule, when it was known as the land of the village of Yen Noi, part of the former Tien Tuc district, which later became Thuong My district. The street's name translates to "Hat Street" due to its historical significance as a center for hat-making and selling various types of hats.

The street's history is deeply rooted in the traditional crafts of Hanoi, with its name "Hang Non" derived from the Vietnamese term for "hat." During the French colonial period, the street was referred to as "rue des Chapeaux," meaning "Hat Street" in French. After the August Revolution in 1945, the street reclaimed its original Vietnamese name, Hang Non, which it has retained ever since.

Today, Hang Non Street is situated within the Hang Gai ward, a bustling area known for its rich cultural heritage and traditional crafts. The street's historical importance extends beyond just its current name, as it was once part of the larger Yen Noi village, which included two distinct areas: Yen Noi Dong Thanh and Yen Noi Co Vu. These areas were named based on their proximity to other neighborhoods such as Dong Thanh and Co Vu, now known as Hang Da.

The eastern segment of what was once Yen Noi Dong Thanh is now known as Ma Vi Street, where artisans produce and sell ceremonial and theatrical accessories used by officials, performers, and worshippers. These include costumes, hats, crowns, flags, fans, and other ritualistic items. The western portion, Hang Non Street, specializes in the production and sale of various types of hats, reflecting the diverse needs of different social classes and professions.

Visitors to Hang Non Street can witness the intricate process of hat-making, which involves meticulous craftsmanship using materials like bamboo, palm leaves, horsehair, and feathers. The hats produced here range from simple straw hats to elaborate ceremonial hats adorned with precious metals and fine embroidery. For instance, the Nón Nghệ, resembling a large wheel with a diameter close to one meter and a height of 10 centimeters, is a unique creation designed for ceremonial purposes. Similarly, the Nón Ba Tầm, a deeper and more complex design, often features ornamental quivers made of silver or gold threads.

The street's historical significance is further highlighted by its role in the early stages of labor movements in Hanoi during the late 1920s. In 1929, the First National Congress of Red Labor Unions was held at number 15 Hang Non Street. This event marked a pivotal moment in the labor movement, bringing together representatives from various regions including Haiphong, Nam Dinh, and the coal mines of Dong Trieu and Mao Ke. The congress adopted a program and charter for the Red Labor Unions, electing Tran Van Lan, a worker from the Nam Dinh textile mill, as the first chairman.

Today, Hang Non Street continues to preserve its rich cultural heritage while also adapting to modern times. Visitors can explore the narrow lanes lined with traditional shops and workshops, each dedicated to the art of hat-making. The atmosphere is filled with the sounds of craftsmen working diligently, the smell of freshly woven materials, and the sight of beautifully crafted hats displayed in windows and stalls. This blend of tradition and contemporary life makes Hang Non Street a fascinating destination for tourists interested in experiencing the authentic culture and craftsmanship of Hanoi.

Hang Non Street, also known as Rue des Chapeaux, has a history that traces back to the French colonial era. Even after the revolution, the street retained its original name, preserving a legacy that resonates through time. The street's origins are rooted in two distinct sections that were later merged into one. The eastern part was originally named Phố Mã Vĩ, dedicated to crafting and selling accessories for officials, performers, and religious ceremonies. This included items such as ceremonial robes, hats, banners, fans, and other paraphernalia used in theater performances and rituals. The name "Mã Vĩ" likely derived from the materials used in making theatrical props, particularly horsehair, which was integral to creating certain types of hats and stage decorations.

The western segment, now known as Hang Non Street, specialized in producing various types of hats. These ranged from sturdy straw hats suitable for farmers to more elaborate military-style helmets. Each hat was meticulously crafted using traditional techniques, with some hats featuring decorative elements like metal tips or ornamental bindings. For instance, the nón lính, resembling a large dish-shaped helmet, was made from woven bamboo strips and featured a pointed metal tip at the center, often secured tightly under the chin with a strap.

Today, Hang Non Street is not solely dedicated to hat-making but retains its historical charm and cultural significance. The street's transformation over the years reflects the dynamic nature of urban development in Hanoi. Despite no longer being a hub for hat production, it remains a vibrant area filled with shops offering a variety of goods. At the end of the street, visitors can find numerous stores selling wooden clogs in various styles, catering to different tastes and needs. Additionally, there are shops that sell traditional paper lanterns and other decorative items, blending seamlessly with the historical ambiance of the area.

One notable landmark on Hang Non Street is the ancient temple of Yên Nội-Đồng Thành, located at number 42. This temple serves as a testament to the historical importance of the area, connecting the present-day neighborhood to its past. The street's historical significance extends beyond its commercial activities, as evidenced by the fact that it was once the site of significant political gatherings. In the summer of 1920, the first National Red Trade Union Congress was held at number 15 Hang Non Street, hosted by Nguyen Duc Canh. Despite these challenges, the street managed to maintain its identity and resilience, adapting to changing times while preserving its unique character.

During the First Indochina War, Hang Non Street, situated within the central region of Hanoi, remained relatively unscathed compared to other areas. Its strategic location between key administrative zones helped shield it from extensive damage, allowing it to retain much of its pre-war appearance and functionality.
