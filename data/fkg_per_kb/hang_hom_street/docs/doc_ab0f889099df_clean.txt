Hang Hom Street, a narrow and charming lane nestled within the historic heart of Hanoi, stands as a testament to the city's rich cultural heritage and enduring charm. Known for its quaint beauty and storied past, this street is one of the 36 ancient streets that form the nucleus of the Old Quarter, each bearing the name of a traditional craft or trade practiced there. The term "Hanoi - 36 streets" is widely recognized among locals, encapsulating the essence of the bustling commercial district that has thrived since the Tran Dynasty, when the area was home to 61 administrative units. By the time of the Le Dynasty, this number had been reduced to 36, a configuration that remained unchanged for nearly three centuries.

Hang Hom Street, with its length of just 120 meters and width of only 6 meters, is one of the smallest yet most significant streets in the Old Quarter. Its compactness makes it easy to traverse, allowing visitors to quickly move from Hang Quat Street to Hang Ga Street, passing through Hang Hom Street in mere steps. Despite its diminutive size, the street is a vital part of the Old Quarter’s intricate network of lanes and alleys, each contributing to the vibrant tapestry of Hanoi’s history and culture.

The street’s origins trace back to the early 19th century when residents from Ha Vi village, located in what is now Thanh Oai District, moved to the area known as Cau Vu Thang, part of the former Tien Tu ward. These settlers brought with them their traditional craft of making wooden boxes coated with lacquer, a skill they had inherited from generations past. Over time, the street became renowned for producing various types of lacquered wooden boxes, including those used for storing clothes and stationery, as well as more ornate pieces adorned with intricate designs and vibrant colors. This legacy continues today, with artisans still crafting these exquisite items, albeit with modern techniques and materials that make the products lighter and more appealing to contemporary tastes.

The street’s name, Hang Hom, reflects its primary function as a marketplace for these lacquered boxes, which were essential household items in old Hanoi. The lacquer boxes, or hom, were not only practical but also beautifully crafted, often featuring elaborate designs and vibrant colors. The production process involved meticulous work, from selecting the wood to applying layers of lacquer and allowing it to dry before polishing it to a glossy finish. This labor-intensive process required skilled craftsmen who could handle every step of the creation, from design to completion.

In addition to the lacquered boxes, Hang Hom Street is also known for its production of lacquerware, including small decorative items such as flower pots, incense burners, and even ceremonial chairs. The lacquer used in these items can be either black or colored, with intricate patterns and designs that reflect the artistic traditions of Vietnam. The artisans here have preserved the traditional methods of lacquer application, which involve multiple layers of lacquer being applied and dried over time, creating a durable and beautiful finish.

The architecture of Hang Hom Street is a blend of old and new, with many of the buildings dating back several decades, some even older. The houses are typically two-story structures with narrow facades, built close together along both sides of the street. The walls of these buildings often show signs of age, with cracks and wear visible, testifying to the passage of time. Many of the houses feature traditional Vietnamese architectural elements, such as ornate wooden doors and windows, and tiled roofs that add to the street’s charm. The atmosphere of the street is one of quiet dignity, with the sounds of footsteps and the occasional chatter of passersby blending harmoniously with the gentle hum of daily life.

Visitors to Hang Hom Street are drawn not only by the historical significance of the place but also by the opportunity to witness the traditional crafts being practiced there. The street retains much of its original character, with many of the shops still operating as family businesses that have been passed down through generations. The artisans continue to produce lacquered boxes and other lacquerware items, often working right within their homes, where the smell of lacquer and the sound of tools can be heard. Visitors can watch as the craftsmen apply layer upon layer of lacquer, carefully smoothing it out until it achieves a perfect shine, a process that requires patience and skill.

For tourists, Hang Hom Street offers a unique glimpse into the traditional crafts of Hanoi, providing a tangible connection to the city’s past. The street is a living museum, where the sights, sounds, and smells of old Hanoi are still very much present. Walking through the narrow lanes, one can almost feel transported back in time, surrounded by the echoes of generations past who lived and worked here. The experience is enhanced by the friendly locals who are often willing to share stories about the street’s history and the traditional crafts practiced there, making each visit a memorable encounter with the soul of Hanoi.

Hang Hom Street, located in Hoan Kiem District of Hanoi, is a historic thoroughfare that stretches for 127 meters and is 6 meters wide. The street runs from the end of Hang Quat Street to the end of Hang Gai Street, connecting two vibrant areas of the Old Quarter. This narrow lane has a rich history dating back to before the French colonial era, when it was known as Rue des Cerceuils. On January 22, 1891, a devastating fire broke out in this area, consuming not only Hang Hom but also several other nearby streets such as Rue des Bambous (Hang Tre), Rue des Radeaux (Hang Manh), Rue des Sceaux (Hang Thung), Rue de la Saumure (Hang Mam), Rue des Changeurs (Hang Bac), Rue du Pont en Bois (Cau Go), and Rue de la Chaux (Hang Voi). The conflagration destroyed 208 houses, including four temples, and caused significant damage to the local community. One notable victim was a Chinese cart owner who lost 60 carts in the blaze. After the fire, the street was renamed Rue des Caisses by the French, and in 1945, it reclaimed its original Vietnamese name, Hang Hom. Further name changes occurred in 1949 and 1951, but the street retained its original title.

The origins of Hang Hom Street can be traced back to the mid-19th century when residents from Ha Vi Village, known for their woodworking skills, moved to this area to establish shops making coffins and chests. Initially, they produced simple wooden boxes and trunks used for storing clothes and documents. Over time, they began crafting more elaborate wooden furniture, which can still be seen today. The craftsmen from Ha Vi Village established a communal temple, called the Ha Vi Temple, at number 11 on the street, dedicated to Tran Lu, a revered ancestor of the craft. Tran Lu, born in 1470 and a native of the village of Binh Vuong in Thuan Thanh district, was a prominent figure who taught the art of lacquer work to his fellow villagers. His teachings spread to neighboring villages like Duyen Truong, Ha Thai, and Ha Vi, fostering a thriving community of artisans.

Today, Hang Hom Street remains an integral part of the bustling Old Quarter, situated within Hang Gai Ward. The street's cultural significance extends beyond its physical presence, embodying the rich heritage of Hanoi's traditional crafts. Visitors can explore the quaint shops along the street, where skilled artisans continue to practice the ancient craft of lacquering wood, creating beautiful coffins, chests, and other wooden items. The atmosphere is one of quiet reverence, punctuated by the soft tapping of brushes against lacquer-coated surfaces and the subtle scent of drying lacquer.

Visitors to Hang Hom Street will find themselves immersed in a world where tradition meets modernity. The narrow lanes are lined with small workshops and stores, each preserving the legacy of generations past. The architecture of the buildings reflects the colonial influence, with many structures featuring French-style facades adorned with intricate ironwork and ornamental details. Despite the passage of time, the essence of Hang Hom Street remains unchanged, offering a glimpse into the enduring spirit of Hanoi's artisanal traditions.
