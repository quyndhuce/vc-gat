In Vietnamese cuisine, Banh mi, also known as bánh mì or bánh mỳ, is a type of short baguette with a thin, crisp crust and a soft, airy texture. This culinary creation, akin to a submarine sandwich, is often split lengthwise and filled with various meats and savory ingredients, serving as a meal known as bánh mì thịt. Plain bánh mì is also consumed as a staple food.

A typical Vietnamese bánh mì is a fusion of native Vietnamese cuisine and French influences. It usually includes proteins and vegetables such as chả lụa (Vietnamese sausage), coriander (cilantro), cucumber, pickled carrots, and pickled daikon, combined with condiments from French cuisine like pâté, red chili, and mayonnaise. However, a variety of popular fillings are used, including xá xíu (Chinese barbecued pork), xíu mại (Vietnamese minced pork), nem nướng (grilled pork sausage), đậu hũ (tofu), and even ice cream, which is more of a dessert option. In Vietnam, these bread rolls and sandwiches are typically eaten for breakfast or as a snack.

The baguette, a key component of Banh mi, was introduced to Vietnam by the French in the mid-19th century during the Nguyễn dynasty. By the early 20th century, it had become a staple food. In the 1950s, a distinctively Vietnamese style of sandwich emerged in Saigon, known as bánh mì Sài Gòn or 'Saigon sandwich'. This style became a popular street food. Following the Vietnam War, overseas Vietnamese communities popularized the bánh mì sandwich in countries such as Australia, Canada, and the United States, where it is commonly sold in Asian bakeries.

The term Banh mi is derived from the Vietnamese words bánh (referring to baked goods, primarily bread) and mì ("wheat"). In northern Vietnam, it is sometimes spelled bánh mỳ. To differentiate between plain bread and the sandwich with fillings, the term bánh mì không ("plain bread") is used. Similarly, to distinguish Vietnamese-style bread from others, terms like bánh mì Sài Gòn ("Saigon-style bread") or bánh mì Việt Nam ("Vietnam-style bread") are employed.

The word bánh mì, meaning "bread," appeared in Vietnamese as early as the 1830s, according to Jean-Louis Taberd's dictionary. The French introduced the baguette and other baked goods like pâté chaud to Vietnam in the 1860s, marking the beginning of French imperialism there. Initially, Northern Vietnamese called the baguette bánh tây, or "Western bánh," while Southern Vietnamese called it bánh mì, "wheat bánh." Nguyễn Đình Chiểu mentioned the baguette in his 1861 poem "Văn tế nghĩa sĩ Cần Giuộc."

Due to the high cost of imported wheat, French baguettes and sandwiches were considered luxuries. During World War I, an influx of French soldiers and supplies, coupled with disruptions in wheat imports, led bakers to mix in cheaper rice flour, making the bread fluffier and more accessible to ordinary Vietnamese. Baguettes were mainly eaten for breakfast with butter and sugar. Sandwiches in the early days followed French tastes, featuring ham and butter moistened with mayonnaise or liver pâté.
