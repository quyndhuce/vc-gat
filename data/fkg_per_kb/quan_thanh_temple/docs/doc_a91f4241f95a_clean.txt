Quan Thanh Temple, also known as Trấn Vũ Temple, is a significant Taoist temple located in Hanoi, Vietnam. Founded during the reign of Emperor Lý Thái Tổ in the 11th century, the temple is dedicated to Trấn Vũ, the deity of the North in Taoism, symbolized by the serpent and turtle. As one of the Four Sacred Temples built to protect the capital from malevolent spirits, Quan Thanh Temple stands guard from the northern direction, with the other three temples positioned to protect from the east, south, and west respectively.

Located near West Lake in Quán Thánh Ward, Quan Thanh Temple is a major tourist attraction in Hanoi. Its name translates to "Place of the Gods," and the long street running by the temple is also called Quán Thánh Street. Over the centuries, the temple has undergone numerous renovations, with the most recent major refurbishment taking place in 1893, which involved the reconstruction of the principal gate and shrine.

The architectural style of Quan Thanh Temple reflects the diverse influences of the imperial era, featuring a large courtyard shaded by a giant banyan tree and a shrine housing a notable bronze statue of Trấn Vũ. This statue, crafted in black bronze by artisans from Ngũ Xã Village in 1677, measures 3.96 meters in height and weighs approximately 3,600 kilograms. It is considered a masterpiece of Vietnamese bronze casting and sculpture, ranking as the second largest bronze statue in Vietnam. Accompanying the statue is a 1.15-meter bronze bell cast simultaneously, both attributed to the master craftsman Trùm Trọng, whose own statue is displayed alongside the deity’s.

Inside the main shrine, visitors can find a valuable collection of ancient texts, including poems and duilians dating back to the 17th and 18th centuries. Each renovation of the temple has been commemorated with a stele, with the oldest dating from 1677 and the most recent from 1894 during the French colonial period under Emperor Thành Thái.

Animal symbolism is prominent within Quan Thanh Temple, particularly the images of the serpent and turtle, which represent wealth and protection respectively. These symbols are derived from a traditional Vietnamese fairy tale where the turtle provides a powerful sword to Lê Thái Tổ, aiding him in victory against the Chinese. Another important symbol is the crane, featured in a story illustrating symbiosis between the crane and the turtle, highlighting their mutual assistance during times of need.

Today, Quan Thanh Temple continues to serve as a site for cultural and religious activities. Visitors can observe the burning of joss paper in the courtyard, especially during Tết, the Lunar New Year, as part of the traditional rituals. Despite the official adoption of the Latin alphabet in the early 20th century, the temple still retains inscriptions in Literary Chinese, reflecting the historical influence of Chinese culture on Vietnamese writing systems.
