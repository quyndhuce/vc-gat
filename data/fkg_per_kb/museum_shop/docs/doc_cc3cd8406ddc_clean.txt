A museum shop, also known as a museum store, is a gift shop typically found within a museum. These shops offer a variety of items such as reproductions of artworks displayed in the museum, picture postcards, books related to the museum's collections, and various souvenirs. Art museums frequently feature clothing and decorative items inspired by or directly replicating the artwork showcased within. Often strategically located near the entrance or exit, these shops serve both as a point of sale and a final destination for visitors.

The idea of a museum gift store began in 1802 at Charles Willson Peale's museum in Philadelphia. Visitors could purchase custom-made silhouette portraits for just eight cents, crafted on-site by Moses Williams, an African-American artist who was formerly enslaved by Peale. This pioneering initiative laid the groundwork for cultural souvenirs, evolving into the retail shops seen today in modern museums. In the United States, museum shops started to develop after World War II, initially small and not professionally managed. However, they gained significant importance as revenue generators in the 1980s.

Today, museum shops play a crucial role in generating income for museums, especially as public funding decreases. They are considered vital components of the visitor experience, often providing educational value alongside financial benefits. Some studies indicate that visitors spend more time in the museum shop, café, and similar areas than they do viewing the actual exhibits. This suggests that the museum shop can enhance the overall visitor experience and engagement with the institution.

Despite their popularity and economic significance, some critics argue against the commercialization of museums, viewing museum shops as potentially undermining the cultural value of the institution. Nevertheless, museum shops remain integral to the perception and operation of museums, contributing significantly to their financial stability and visitor satisfaction.
